WHY YOU SHOULD ACKNOWLEDGE THIS RECORD

1. Silence leaves specific documentary questions unanswered

When a recipient receives a documented account containing identifiable records, dates, correspondence and questions, a refusal to acknowledge it leaves the recipient’s position unknown. I cannot tell whether the evidence has been examined, whether particular documents are disputed, whether the matter falls outside the recipient’s authority or whether the correspondence has simply been set aside. I regard persistent silence in these circumstances as significant because its practical effect may be to leave the person raising concerns without an answer while the existing institutional position remains undisturbed. If you disagree with my account, say so. If you dispute the documents, identify them. If you cannot assist, explain why. Silence is not a reasoned answer. The absence of a response should not be mistaken for the resolution of the questions raised.

2. The published evidence can be examined independently

The archive is intended to allow readers to examine documentary material rather than accept my interpretation on trust. The presence of a document does not automatically prove every conclusion drawn from it. I ask that the primary material be examined in context. If a document has been misinterpreted, identify the passage and explain the correct interpretation. If another record contradicts it, identify that record. If the material is incomplete, specify what is missing. A recipient who believes the conclusions are unsupported has an opportunity to explain the evidentiary gap.

3. A referral is not necessarily a resolution

Where a person is passed between departments, complaint bodies, oversight agencies or service providers, it is necessary to establish whether the underlying questions have actually been examined. A referral may be appropriate, but it does not by itself establish that the substance of the complaint has been addressed. Please identify the receiving authority, the issue referred, the limits of its jurisdiction and whether any further avenue remains available. A chain of referrals can leave a person moving through a system without receiving an answer to the original question.

4. The chronology deserves examination as a whole

What was known at a particular time, what information was available, what action followed and what questions remained unresolved may all matter when assessing institutional conduct. I ask recipients to examine the relevant sequence rather than isolate a single response and treat it as a complete account. If a date, event or interpretation is disputed, identify it. If there is a documented explanation for an apparent inconsistency, provide it. The chronology is offered so judgment can be informed by the sequence of events rather than assumptions or incomplete recollection.

5. Acknowledgment does not require an admission of liability

Confirming receipt of correspondence does not require a recipient to concede wrongdoing, endorse my conclusions or accept legal liability. It establishes that the material has arrived and creates an opportunity to clarify responsibility. A recipient can acknowledge receipt while disputing allegations, identifying missing evidence, explaining jurisdictional limits or stating that no further action will be taken. I should not have to obtain a final determination of every disputed issue merely to know whether my correspondence has been received.

6. Apparent inconsistencies in official records should be explained

Where official documents appear inconsistent, the discrepancy should be identified and examined. Different documents may have been written for different purposes, under different legal powers or on different factual assumptions. There may be a legitimate explanation, or a material gap requiring further inquiry. Identify the relevant records, explain the apparent discrepancy and state whether additional information is required. This is not a demand that every apparent inconsistency be treated as proof of wrongdoing; it is a request that checkable discrepancies be dealt with transparently.

7. A procedural decision does not necessarily answer every substantive

question

A complaint may be declined, closed, restricted or referred on jurisdictional grounds. Such a decision may be valid within the applicable process. But a decision about whether an agency can consider a complaint is not necessarily a finding about whether the underlying events occurred. Please distinguish between the decision made and the factual issues that remain unresolved. If you have considered the substance, identify the scope. If you have not, explain the limitation. If another authority is responsible, identify the basis for that position.

THE SIGNIFICANCE OF SILENCE: WHEN

NON-RESPONSE PROTECTS THE POWERFUL

I anticipate that many recipients will refuse to acknowledge me, just as my experience and pattern recognition over thirty-five years have led me to anticipate. But I want every recipient to understand that, in the circumstances documented in this archive, I do not regard persistent silence as an innocent administrative detail. When powerful people are alleged to have caused serious detriment, and the documentary record is placed before people who have the opportunity to examine it, a refusal to acknowledge the person seeking accountability has consequences. It leaves the existing balance of power undisturbed. It leaves me carrying the burden alone. And it allows the unanswered allegations to remain unanswered.

I believe that powerful, wealthy and corrupt individuals can protect themselves not only through direct action, but through an environment in which other people become unwilling to question them. Some may be motivated by fear, some by loyalty, some by personal advantage, some by prejudice, and some by a desire to preserve their own comfort and standing. In my account, this is how a powerful person?s influence can extend beyond that individual: other people begin reinforcing the established narrative, dismissing the person raising concerns, or refusing to engage with evidence that might challenge the accepted version of events. I see that pattern as a central part of the injustice I am asking recipients to examine.

I use the expression flying monkeys to describe people whom I believe have acted as enablers or defenders of a powerful person?s narrative, whether knowingly or otherwise. I am not claiming that every person who fails to respond has the same motive. I am saying that where people repeatedly refuse to examine evidence, while their silence leaves a vulnerable person isolated and the powerful person?s position unchallenged, their conduct can have the practical effect of protecting that power. Whether they intend that result or not, the result matters.

I am asking recipients to recognise that neutrality cannot simply be declared while its practical effect is to leave one side protected by power and the other side abandoned without an answer. If you have received my documentary evidence and refuse to acknowledge it, you leave me unable to establish whether you have examined the material, whether you dispute it, or whether you have simply decided that engaging with me is too uncomfortable or inconvenient.
