Part II — The central NDIS safeguarding issue

4. The participant’s reported circumstances

The central safeguarding question is: after receiving information that an NDIS participant believed he was unsafe and wanted to leave a provider, what did the provider and the responsible government bodies do to assess the risk, protect the participant and facilitate an appropriate response?

The investigation should establish the date of each request to leave; the recipient of each request; whether the request was acknowledged; whether any risk assessment was completed; whether the participant was offered alternative supports; whether any barrier to changing providers was imposed; whether the barrier had a lawful basis; whether the provider’s financial interests were considered; whether the participant’s views were properly recorded; and whether the Commission or NDIA received the complaints and what action followed.

A provider relationship is not proof that the participant’s needs are being met. Nor does a participant’s continued receipt of services establish that the arrangement remains safe, appropriate or voluntary.

5. NDIS Code of Conduct

The relevant legislation is the National Disability Insurance Scheme Act 2013 (Cth), particularly section 73V, together with section 6 of the National Disability Insurance Scheme (Code of Conduct) Rules 2018.

The Code requires covered persons to respect individual rights to freedom of expression, self-determination and decision-making; respect the privacy of people with disability; provide supports and services safely and competently, with care and skill; act with integrity, honesty and transparency; promptly raise and act on concerns about the quality and safety of supports; take reasonable steps to prevent and respond to violence, exploitation, neglect and abuse; and take reasonable steps to prevent and respond to sexual misconduct.

Section 73V makes compliance with the Code a statutory requirement. The Act provides civil penalties for contravention, including a higher penalty for serious contraventions by NDIS providers.

If the evidence establishes that Ablepoint or a covered worker failed to respect the participant’s decision-making, failed to respond to complaints, provided unsafe supports, disregarded privacy or failed to take reasonable steps to prevent or respond to abuse or neglect, the relevant Code obligations may be engaged. The investigation should not simply ask whether the provider has policies. It should determine whether those policies were followed in the actual circumstances.

The relevant records include service agreements, support plans, risk assessments, case notes, complaints and incident registers, staff communications, provider responses to requests to leave, internal investigations, and records of any referral to the NDIS Commission.

Official sources: NDIS Act. NDIS Code of Conduct.

Part III — Protected disclosures and victimisation

7. NDIS Act protected-disclosure provisions

Sections 73ZA–73ZD of the NDIS Act establish a specific framework for disclosures qualifying for protection. This is particularly important because Dr McLean identifies himself as an NDIS participant who has made complaints about an NDIS provider.

The statutory framework addresses disclosures qualifying for protection; protection from liability for qualifying disclosures; confidentiality of the discloser’s identity; prohibition of victimisation; and compensation where the statutory requirements are satisfied.

The relevant legal question is whether particular disclosures made by Dr McLean met the statutory requirements. For each disclosure, the investigation should identify the information disclosed; the date and method of disclosure; the recipient; whether the recipient was authorised under the Act; whether the disclosure concerned suspected contravention by an NDIS provider; whether the statutory reasonable-grounds requirement was met; whether the disclosure qualified for protection; whether any subsequent detriment or threat of detriment occurred; whether the alleged detriment was connected to the protected disclosure; and whether compensation or another remedy may be available.

The existence of a complaint does not automatically establish victimisation. The required statutory connection must be established. However, a qualifying disclosure must not be treated as an ordinary complaint stripped of its statutory protection.

Required determination: the NDIS Commission and NDIA should identify which disclosures qualified for protection and whether any conduct within their knowledge raises an issue under the victimisation provisions. Official source: NDIS Act.

8. Commonwealth Public Interest Disclosure Act

The Public Interest Disclosure Act 2013 (Cth) provides a separate framework for disclosures concerning wrongdoing or maladministration within the Commonwealth public sector. The current compilation identified for this report is Compilation No. 20, effective 4 June 2026.

Relevant provisions include section 10, protection of disclosers; section 13, what constitutes taking a reprisal; sections 14–16, civil remedies; section 19, reprisal offences; section 20, protection of identifying information; section 29, disclosable conduct; sections 43–45, allocation of disclosures; sections 47–53, investigation arrangements; and sections 59–60, additional obligations of principal officers and authorised officers.

The Act’s objects include promoting integrity and accountability in the Commonwealth public sector and supporting and protecting eligible public officials who make qualifying disclosures. An important limitation is that the Commonwealth PID Act is not a general-purpose whistleblower statute for every member of the public. Eligibility and disclosure requirements must be satisfied.

Therefore, Dr McLean’s disclosures should be assessed individually to determine whether he was eligible to make a protected disclosure under the Act or whether another statutory protection applies. The investigation should not assume that rejection, delay or non-allocation of a disclosure automatically establishes reprisal. It should examine the statutory eligibility requirements, the decision-making record and any evidence of detriment connected with a protected disclosure.

Official source: Public Interest Disclosure Act 2013.
