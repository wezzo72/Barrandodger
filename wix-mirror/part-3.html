Part II — The central NDIS safeguarding issue

4. The participant’s reported circumstances

Dr McLean states that he has repeatedly asked to leave Ablepoint because he believes he has experienced abuse, neglect, surveillance, intimidation, deprivation and unsafe support arrangements.

He alleges that his requests have not resulted in an effective transition to alternative support.

He also alleges that the continuation of the arrangements has contributed to poverty, insecurity, loss of independence and an inability to live with dignity.

These are allegations and testimony requiring examination against the provider’s records and the records of the relevant NDIS bodies.

The central safeguarding question is:

After receiving information that an NDIS participant believed he was unsafe and wanted to leave a provider, what did the provider and the responsible government bodies do to assess the risk, protect the participant and facilitate an appropriate response?

The investigation should establish:

  1. The date of each request to leave.
  2. The recipient of each request.
  3. Whether the request was acknowledged.
  4. Whether any risk assessment was completed.
  5. Whether the participant was offered alternative supports.
  6. Whether any barrier to changing providers was imposed.
  7. Whether the barrier had a lawful basis.
  8. Whether the provider’s financial interests were considered.
  9. Whether the participant’s views were properly recorded.
  10. Whether the Commission or NDIA received the complaints and what action followed.

A provider relationship is not proof that the participant’s needs are being met. Nor does a participant’s continued receipt of services establish that the arrangement remains safe, appropriate or voluntary.

5. NDIS Code of Conduct

The relevant legislation is the National Disability Insurance Scheme Act 2013 (Cth), particularly section 73V, together with section 6 of the National Disability Insurance Scheme (Code of Conduct) Rules 2018.

The Code requires covered persons to:

  • Respect individual rights to freedom of expression, self-determination and decision-making.
  • Respect the privacy of people with disability.
  • Provide supports and services safely and competently, with care and skill.
  • Act with integrity, honesty and transparency.
  • Promptly raise and act on concerns about the quality and safety of supports.
  • Take reasonable steps to prevent and respond to violence, exploitation, neglect and abuse.
  • Take reasonable steps to prevent and respond to sexual misconduct.

Section 73V makes compliance with the Code a statutory requirement. The Act provides civil penalties for contravention, including a higher penalty for serious contraventions by NDIS providers.

Potential application to the reported circumstances. If the evidence establishes that Ablepoint or a covered worker failed to respect the participant’s decision-making, failed to respond to complaints, provided unsafe supports, disregarded privacy or failed to take reasonable steps to prevent or respond to abuse or neglect, the relevant Code obligations may be engaged.

The investigation should not simply ask whether the provider has policies. It should determine whether those policies were followed in the actual circumstances.

The relevant records include:

  • Service agreements.
  • Support plans.
  • Risk assessments.
  • Case notes.
  • Complaints and incident registers.
  • Staff communications.
  • Provider responses to requests to leave.
  • Internal investigations.
  • Records of any referral to the NDIS Commission.

Official sources:

  • NDIS Act: https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2013A00020/latest
  • NDIS Code of Conduct: https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2018L00629/latest

6. Complaints and incident management

The NDIS Act also contains provisions concerning registered-provider complaints and incident management.

Relevant provisions include:

  • Section 73W — complaints management and resolution systems.
  • Section 73X — arrangements relating to complaints management and resolution.
  • Section 73Y — incident management systems.

The investigation should establish whether the participant’s complaints were properly recorded, assessed, investigated and resolved.

The Commission should identify the complaint and incident records it holds and explain the action taken in relation to each matter.

Part III — Protected disclosures and victimisation

7. NDIS Act protected-disclosure provisions

Sections 73ZA–73ZD of the NDIS Act establish a specific framework for disclosures qualifying for protection.

This is particularly important because Dr McLean identifies himself as an NDIS participant who has made complaints about an NDIS provider.

The statutory framework addresses:

  • Disclosures qualifying for protection.
  • Protection from liability for qualifying disclosures.
  • Confidentiality of the discloser’s identity.
  • Prohibition of victimisation.
  • Compensation where the statutory requirements are satisfied.

The relevant legal question is whether particular disclosures made by Dr McLean met the statutory requirements.

For each disclosure, the investigation should identify:

  1. The information disclosed.
  2. The date and method of disclosure.
  3. The recipient.
  4. Whether the recipient was authorised under the Act.
  5. Whether the disclosure concerned suspected contravention by an NDIS provider.
  6. Whether the statutory reasonable-grounds requirement was met.
  7. Whether the disclosure qualified for protection.
  8. Whether any subsequent detriment or threat of detriment occurred.
  9. Whether the alleged detriment was connected to the protected disclosure.
  10. Whether compensation or another remedy may be available.

The existence of a complaint does not automatically establish victimisation. The required statutory connection must be established.

However, a qualifying disclosure must not be treated as an ordinary complaint stripped of its statutory protection.

Required determination: The NDIS Commission and NDIA should identify which disclosures qualified for protection and whether any conduct within their knowledge raises an issue under the victimisation provisions.

Official source: https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2013A00020/latest

8. Commonwealth Public Interest Disclosure Act

The Public Interest Disclosure Act 2013 (Cth) provides a separate framework for disclosures concerning wrongdoing or maladministration within the Commonwealth public sector.

The current compilation identified for this report is Compilation No. 20, effective 4 June 2026.

Relevant provisions include:

  • Section 10 — protection of disclosers.
  • Section 13 — what constitutes taking a reprisal.
  • Sections 14–16 — civil remedies.
  • Section 19 — reprisal offences.
  • Section 20 — protection of identifying information.
  • Section 29 — disclosable conduct.
  • Sections 43–45 — allocation of disclosures.
  • Sections 47–53 — investigation arrangements.
  • Sections 59–60 — additional obligations of principal officers and authorised officers.

The Act’s objects include promoting integrity and accountability in the Commonwealth public sector and supporting and protecting eligible public officials who make qualifying disclosures.

An important limitation is that the Commonwealth PID Act is not a general-purpose whistleblower statute for every member of the public. Eligibility and disclosure requirements must be satisfied.

Therefore, Dr McLean’s disclosures should be assessed individually to determine whether he was eligible to make a protected disclosure under the Act or whether another statutory protection applies.

The investigation should not assume that rejection, delay or non-allocation of a disclosure automatically establishes reprisal. It should examine the statutory eligibility requirements, the decision-making record and any evidence of detriment connected with a protected disclosure.

Official source: https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2013A00133/latest

Part VI — Financial independence and NSW Trustee and Guardian

15. NSW Trustee and Guardian statutory principles

Section 39 of the NSW Trustee and Guardian Act 2009 (NSW) sets out general principles applicable to functions under the relevant Chapter.

These principles include:

  • Giving paramount consideration to the welfare and interests of the person.
  • Restricting freedom of decision and freedom of action as little as possible.
  • Encouraging the person to live a normal life in the community.
  • Taking the person’s views into consideration.
  • Recognising the importance of preserving family relationships and cultural and linguistic environments.
  • Encouraging self-reliance in personal, domestic and financial affairs.
  • Protecting the person from neglect, abuse and exploitation.

These principles are relevant where an applicable financial management or guardianship arrangement affects the participant’s independence.

The investigation must first identify the precise order and the statutory authority under which it was made.

A financial management order and a guardianship order are not interchangeable. Their legal effects must not be assumed.

The investigation should obtain:

  1. The original order.
  2. The court or tribunal that made it.
  3. The date and statutory basis.
  4. The reasons for the order.
  5. The scope of the appointed person’s authority.
  6. Any instructions to financial institutions.
  7. Any requests by Dr McLean for access to funds.
  8. The response to those requests.
  9. Any review or variation applications.
  10. Any consideration of less restrictive arrangements.

Where access to money for food, medication, accommodation or other essential needs is affected, the institution should explain the legal basis and practical arrangements for meeting those needs.

The relevant question is whether the restrictions were lawful, necessary, proportionate and consistent with the statutory principles.

Part VII — Legal Aid and access to justice

16. Legal Aid NSW

Dr McLean reports that Legal Aid NSW restricted or refused legal assistance in circumstances involving disability support, financial restrictions, provider complaints and police proceedings.

He regards this as an inversion of the purpose of access to justice: the person seeking to challenge alleged wrongdoing may be unable to obtain the assistance needed to do so.

That concern warrants examination, but the legal analysis must distinguish between a refusal that is lawful under eligibility and merits criteria and a refusal that is affected by error, unfairness, discrimination or failure to consider relevant circumstances.

Important statutory correction: Section 56 of the Legal Aid Commission Act 1979 (NSW) should not be cited as a general appeal provision for refusal of legal aid. The current section 56 concerns the Chief Executive Officer. The actual review or appeal rights must be identified from the relevant decision, the applicable provisions and Legal Aid NSW’s current procedures.

The investigation should obtain:

  • The original application.
  • The decision notice.
  • The date and identity of the decision-maker.
  • The reasons for refusal or restriction.
  • The policy and criteria applied.
  • Any relevant assessment of means and merits.
  • Any consideration of disability-related communication needs.
  • Any internal review or reconsideration request.
  • Any advice about external review rights.

The practical effect of denying legal assistance may be serious where a person is seeking to challenge decisions affecting liberty, safety, money, accommodation or disability support.

That practical effect should be recognised without alleging, in the absence of evidence, that Legal Aid intended to protect criminal conduct or a criminal enterprise.

The investigation should determine whether the decision was properly made, whether the applicant was informed of available review options and whether disability-related adjustments were considered.
