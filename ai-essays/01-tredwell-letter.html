01 — What the 27 March 2023 Federal Court letter decides

Executive proposition

On 27 March 2023, Scott Tredwell, General Counsel of the Federal Court of Australia, wrote to Dr Rich McLean and decided not to allocate a public-interest disclosure. The letter is an authorised-officer assessment under the Public Interest Disclosure Act 2013 (Cth). It is not a judgment of the Court, and it does not confirm that a protected disclosure was made.

Scope

The document examined is the letter that begins on the Federal Court letterhead inside a 27-page PDF published in the Backup repository. The surrounding pages are archive covers and donation panels. Those covers were read and are treated as archive additions, not as the letter.

Documentary findings

The letter is dated 27 March 2023, addressed to Dr Rich McLean at richarddrawstuff@gmail.com, and refers to correspondence of 7 March, 23 March and 24 March 2023. Mr Tredwell states that he is an officer authorised to carry out authorised-officer functions under the PID Act.

He records that, on the information provided to him, he is satisfied that the correspondent is, or was, an employee with the Department of Social Services, trading as Rich McLean, Arts Life Coach, Peer-Support Worker and Mental Health Advocate.

He then states that he is satisfied the disclosed conduct does not relate in any way to the Federal Court, the Federal Circuit and Family Court of Australia, or the National Native Title Tribunal, and that he is not satisfied the disclosure was provided to an authorised recipient.

He records the correspondent’s belief that the information tends to show conduct within three PID Act categories: perverting the course of justice, maladministration, and danger to health or safety. He writes that it is difficult to determine whether that belief is on reasonable grounds, and that for the initial assessment he is prepared to assume the conduct is disclosable.

The decision paragraph then says he is not satisfied the disclosure was made by a public official or provided to an authorised recipient, so there is no reasonable basis to decide that it is an internal disclosure. He decides not to allocate the disclosure to any agency. He states that no further action under the PID Act will be taken by the Federal Court or the FCFCOA on that material, and that this does not affect what another agency might do with a disclosure made to that agency.

Primary evidence

27 March 2023 letter of Scott Tredwell, General Counsel (the letter text is inside the PDF; earlier pages are archive covers).

Corroboration

The Administrative Appeals Tribunal later treated a copy of this letter as Exhibit A1 in McLean and Comcare, file 2021/7478. That is independent in the limited sense that a tribunal recorded the letter’s existence and quoted the employment sentence. It is not independent proof that the employment sentence is correct. The Tribunal gave the opinion little weight. See essay 02.

Repeating the letter on archive pages is derivative, not corroboration.

Contrary evidence

The same letter contains both the sentence accepting DSS employment on the information provided, and the decision sentence that he is not satisfied the disclosure was made by a public official or made to an authorised recipient. Those sentences are not reconciled inside the letter.

The PDF’s cover pages call the person a “Federal-Court-confirmed Protected Disclosure maker.” The letter itself refuses allocation and says no further PID Act action will be taken by the Court on that disclosure. The cover claim is archive interpretation. It is not the letter’s decision.

The Tribunal later recorded a 31 March 2023 Department of Social Services email, Exhibit R3, as saying the Department has no record of him ever being its employee. This synthesis did not open Exhibit R3 itself. What is established is that the Tribunal said that.

What the record establishes

• A — A letter dated 27 March 2023, signed Scott Tredwell, General Counsel, decides not to allocate the disclosure.

• A — The letter states the three assumptions above, and states that the conduct described does not relate to the Federal Court, FCFCOA or NNTT.

• A — The letter contains the DSS-employment sentence quoted below, expressly based on information the correspondent provided.

• C — The underlying allegations of perverting justice, maladministration, and danger remain allegations. The letter assumes them only for the initial assessment and does not find them proved.

What the record does not establish

It does not establish that a court found disclosable conduct, that whistleblower protections were granted, that the correspondent was a DSS employee as a matter of law, or that any person committed a crime. It does not establish the cover-page claims about Bitcoin sealing, download counts, or an ICC case.

Unresolved questions

Why the decision paragraph denies public-official status after the earlier paragraph accepts DSS employment on the information provided is not explained in the letter. Whether another agency later allocated a different disclosure is outside this letter.

Evidence classification

Employment sentence, as a sentence in the letter: A (the sentence exists). Employment as legal fact: not established by this letter; see essay 02, where a tribunal was not satisfied.

Allocation refusal: A, official record of an authorised officer, not a judicial determination.

Cover-page “confirmed protected disclosure maker”: E as fact; it is archive interpretation contradicted by the decision paragraph.

Conclusion

A third party can conclude that the Federal Court’s General Counsel refused to allocate this disclosure and said the Court would take no further PID Act action on it. A third party cannot conclude from this letter that the Court confirmed a protected disclosure or upheld the allegations.

Quoted decision language, from the extracted letter: “I must decide … not to allocate the handling of that disclosure to any agency.” And: “This means that no further action under the PID Act will be taken by the Federal Court or FCFCOA.”
