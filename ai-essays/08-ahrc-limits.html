08 — What the AHRC said it could not do on 4 July 2023

Executive proposition

On 4 July 2023 the Australian Human Rights Commission’s Investigation and Conciliation Service wrote that it does not have power to grant “whistleblower status,” that being a whistleblower is not a protected attribute under the anti-discrimination laws it administers, and that “gangstalking” is not a ground of discrimination in that jurisdiction. The email explains limits. It does not find the underlying allegations proved or disproved.

Scope

An email from InfoService@humanrights.gov.au to Rich McLean. Thirty-seven pages were in the PDF. This essay uses the jurisdictional passages on the first pages. It does not treat every attachment he sent as having been re-read.

Documentary findings

The email thanks him and notes he wants whistleblower protections because of reporting corrupt practices, including a request that the AHRC initiate procedures to grant whistleblower status. It says the most the Investigation and Conciliation Service can offer is dispute resolution and conciliation, with very few enforceable powers, and that the power to grant whistleblower status is arguably not a power the AHRC has. It says being a whistleblower is arguably not a protected attribute such as sex, age or race.

It refers to letters he supplied, including one from Tahsinullah Sultani, about disability advocacy, homelessness, starvation and denial of financial support. This synthesis did not open the Sultani letter. The AHRC email says complaints must be by or on behalf of a person aggrieved, in an area the Commission covers.

It says human-rights complaints under the Australian Human Rights Commission Act 1986 (Cth) have to identify the instrument and the Commonwealth body. It says the Commission cannot deal with state agencies or private bodies in that human-rights jurisdiction, and that the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights is not a scheduled instrument.

On “gangstalking,” it says that is not a discrimination ground and it is not clear the issue is within the scheduled human-rights jurisdiction. It says examples he gave — AGIS not investigating a former partner, and an ASIO employee who owes a settlement threatening to kill him and his dog — need names, dates, context, and whether the matter was reported. It points complaints about federal agencies to the Commonwealth Ombudsman. It does not find that those events occurred or did not occur.

It says victimisation under its laws has a specific meaning tied to detriment because a person lodged a discrimination complaint or asserted rights the Commission covers. On the information provided, it was not clear the asserted detriment was for that reason, as distinct from being regarded as a whistleblower.

It says the Commission cannot order a remedy. If conciliation fails and the President finds a human-rights breach, the President may report to the Attorney-General with recommendations. The Commission does not review courts or tribunals.
