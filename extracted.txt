The following section expands the documentary basis of my testimony. It does not replace the 
statement above, and it does not ask the reader to accept every inference as an established 
finding. It identifies records and record-groups visible in the public Barrandodger repository and 
explains what should be tested by reading the underlying documents in full. The repository’s 
public file index and the supplied tree-export data are finding aids: they help locate files, paths, 
versions and Git objects. A filename, a file hash, a timestamp or a letterhead may assist with 
provenance and identification, but none alone proves the truth of every statement contained in a 
document or establishes the legal responsibility of a person or institution.

WHAT HASHES, COMMITS AND TIMESTAMPS DO — AND DO NOT — SHOW
A Git blob identifier can help identify the content object associated with a file in a particular 
repository tree. A commit history can show that repository changes were recorded over time. A

cryptographic hash can help establish whether a file later examined is byte-for-byte identical to a 
file with a previously recorded hash. An OpenTimestamps receipt may help establish that a 
particular hash was submitted to a timestamping system by a particular time, subject to 
verification of the receipt and its proof chain. These are provenance and preservation tools. They 
are not proof that the document’s author was correct, that an allegation is true, that an agency 
acted unlawfully, or that a named person committed a crime. The evidentiary value is 
strengthened when the original file, its source, the full context, the timestamp receipt and a 
reproducible verification method are retained together.
The “Jason code data (1).pdf” tree export should therefore be treated as a technical locator. Its 
paths, object identifiers, sizes and API URLs can help identify which file was present in the 
exported tree and how a reviewer might retrieve it. They should be checked against the current 
repository and, where material, a preserved copy of the relevant commit. If the same filename 
appears in more than one directory, or several similarly named copies exist, the reviewer should 
compare file contents and hashes rather than assume that all copies are identical. A renamed file 
may be the same content, and two files with similar names may be different versions. The record 
should preserve that distinction rather than silently deduplicate or overwrite material.
THE DOCUMENTARY TRAP: THE CENTRAL PROPOSITION
The repository contains “THE DOCUMENTARY TRAP WHEN THE SYSTEM’S OWN RECORD 
BECOMES THE EVIDENCE.pdf.” The title expresses the central proposition of this testimony: 
institutional correspondence, decisions, classifications, referrals, reasons and recorded omissions 
may become material for assessing administrative conduct when read together over time. The 
essay’s existence establishes that this interpretation has been published and preserved; it does 
not itself establish every conclusion advanced in it.
The question is not whether one isolated letter proves an entire theory of events. The question is 
whether a series of dated records can be assembled into a reliable chronology; whether later 
decisions are consistent with earlier positions; whether different agencies give compatible 
explanations; whether a stated referral or action is followed by a traceable outcome; and whether 
any apparent gap or contradiction can be resolved by obtaining the missing record. A fair review 
must also allow for ordinary administrative explanations, jurisdictional limits, incomplete files, 
errors, changed circumstances, privacy constraints and differences between a complaint, a 
preliminary assessment, an administrative decision and a finding after a contested process.
That is why I ask readers to examine the records rather than simply accept either my conclusions 
or an institution’s characterisation of me. A document can establish that a statement was made or 
a decision was issued without automatically proving every assertion within it. Equally, a 
complaint or submission can be relevant even if it is not a final finding: it may establish what 
concern was raised, what information was supplied, when an institution was notified and what 
response followed. The correct analysis identifies precisely what each record establishes and 
what remains unproved.

THE DOCUMENTARY TRAP-When the Official Record Becomes the Evidence
There is a paradox at the centre of my experience.
For decades, I was repeatedly placed in the position of having to prove what had 
happened to me.
I was required to explain myself to governments, agencies, police, lawyers, courts, 
tribunals, health services, regulators, professional bodies and other institutions. Each 
new dispute generated another complaint, another letter, another assessment, another 
decision, another referral, another administrative classification or another official 
response.
I kept the records.
And that is where the paradox begins.
The institutions that required me to provide evidence also generated an enormous 
proportion of the documentary record through which my account can now be examined.
I describe this as the Documentary Trap.
I do not claim that the existence of these records, by itself, proves that a government 
secretly designed a coordinated trap for me. That would require direct evidence of 
intention and coordination.
The proposition is both stronger and more defensible:
The machinery of administration created a documentary record that can now be 
examined against the institutions themselves.
The system demanded evidence.
I preserved it.
The system generated records.
I preserved those too.
The result is that the evidentiary burden can no longer be framed solely as:
“Why should anyone believe Richard McLean?”
The question becomes:
“What do the institutions’ own records establish?”
That is a fundamentally different question.

I. THE PERSON WAS MADE THE EVIDENCE
The easiest way to dismiss an individual complaint is to make the individual the entire 
evidentiary object.
Was the person credible?
Was the account accurate?
Was the complaint exaggerated?
Was there corroboration?
Was the person confused?
Was there sufficient independent evidence?
Those questions can place an extraordinary burden upon an individual.
But the evidentiary situation changes when the individual produces the institutions’ 
own correspondence.
An allegation contained only in a personal statement remains an allegation.
A government letter establishes that the government made that communication.
A court filing establishes that the filing occurred.
A complaint establishes that a complaint was made.
A decision establishes that a decision was made.
A referral establishes that a referral occurred.
An acknowledgement establishes receipt or processing.
A later document can then be compared with an earlier document.
That comparison is the beginning of documentary accountability.
The archive’s own methodology expressly adopts this distinction. It classifies material 
as documented fact, corroborated claim, documented allegation, inference, 
interpretation or unresolved matter rather than treating every statement as equivalent. 
[E1]
That distinction is crucial.
It means the archive does not need to claim that every proposition is already proved.
The archive can instead say:
Here is the document.
Here is who created it.
Here is when it was created.
Here is what it says.
Here is what happened afterwards.
Here is the later document.
Here is the contradiction or continuity.
Here is the institutional response.
Here is what remains unresolved.
That is a much more demanding form of accountability.
