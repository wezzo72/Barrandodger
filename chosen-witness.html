III

Isaiah and the language of being chosen

The title of this work deliberately invokes biblical witness. In Isaiah 43:10, in the King James Version, the language of witnesses and of being chosen appears inside God’s address to Israel. The passage belongs to a prophetic argument about God’s identity, Israel’s vocation and the testimony of God’s works. For Barrandodger, the scripture offers a vocabulary through which testimony is not merely optional self-expression but a responsibility. The distinction between the original text and its personal application must remain explicit. Isaiah 43:10 does not name Barrandodger. It does not provide documentary proof that a particular contemporary person has been divinely appointed. Applying its language to my life is a theological interpretation arising from Christian faith. A theological interpretation can have profound personal and ethical meaning without being mistaken for an independently verifiable historical fact.

The question, then, is what the calling requires of the person who claims it. If

I believe I am called to witness, I must take truth seriously. If I believe I am called to preserve testimony, I must preserve the original records rather than merely my preferred summaries. If I believe I am called to pursue justice, I must distinguish accountability from revenge. If I believe my testimony matters, I must allow it to be examined. The vocation of witness is not a licence to make unsupported accusations. It is a demand to bear testimony responsibly.

I can say that I believe God has called me to this work. I can explain how scripture informs that belief. I can publish the records on which my historical arguments depend. IV

No weapon formed, and the limits of that promise

For a person who interprets his life through Christianity, the verse can express a determination not to allow adversity to determine the final meaning of his existence. The distinction matters because a serious witness must be capable of acknowledging an adverse finding without surrendering the right to ask further questions. It is measured by its capacity to remain honest under scrutiny. V

Courage without infallibility

Romans 8:31 asks, if God be for us, who can be against us? Within Paul’s argument the question belongs to a discussion of God’s love, suffering and the hope of believers. It is not a claim that Christians never encounter opposition, legal setbacks, misunderstanding or injustice. For Barrandodger the verse can express a conviction that adversity need not extinguish purpose.

A person may be challenged without being silenced. A person may be criticised without becoming worthless. A person may be mistaken about a particular detail without losing the right to tell the truth about other experiences. A person may encounter an adverse decision and still have legitimate questions about its scope, reasoning or consequences. This is the distinction between spiritual confidence and factual infallibility. I can believe that God sustains me without claiming that God has independently verified every sentence I write. I can believe that my life has a purpose without asserting that all disagreement proves hostility. I can believe that truth matters without presuming that I alone possess it in its entirety. The prophetic challenge becomes more powerful when it accepts that distinction. My faith is the foundation of my declaration. The evidence is the basis on which my factual claims must be assessed. An assisted account that pretends otherwise would betray both the faith and the archive.

VII

Justice, mercy, humility, and the ethics of

anger

Micah 6:8 places justice, mercy and humility together. The verse is indispensable to a prophetic narrative that speaks with anger. Anger may arise from experiences a person understands as humiliating, unjust or dehumanising. It can motivate someone to preserve records, challenge a decision or insist that a complaint be properly addressed. Anger cannot establish the truth of an allegation on its own.

Justice requires evidence. Mercy requires the recognition of human dignity. Humility requires a willingness to acknowledge uncertainty and revise conclusions when warranted. These principles are not enemies of accountability. They are safeguards against turning accountability into personal vengeance. If I demand that institutions be precise in describing my conduct, I must also be precise when describing theirs. I must identify what I know, what I believe, what I infer and what remains unverified. I must distinguish the conduct established by a document from the motive I suspect. I must not transform an unanswered question into a proven conspiracy. I must not claim that a person’s appearance in a record establishes guilt.

Amos 5:24 uses the image of justice flowing like water and righteousness like a mighty stream. In its setting it denounces religious observance that is not accompanied by the justice God requires. Applied here, it demands that institutions be judged by conduct rather than by the formal descriptions they give of themselves. A policy may promise fairness. A procedure may describe a route for complaints. The relevant question is how those commitments operate in practice. To establish that a process failed, the researcher must identify the applicable commitment, the event, and the discrepancy between them. To establish that a person deliberately violated a duty, further evidence may be required. The prophetic language gives moral significance to the inquiry. It does not remove the need to conduct it.

Proverbs 18:17 observes that the first account in a dispute may seem convincing until another comes and examines it. An initial account may be

sincere yet incomplete. An official explanation may be accurate within its scope yet omit matters outside that scope. A complainant may preserve important evidence while drawing conclusions that require further testing. The remedy is not to decide in advance which speaker deserves belief. It is to examine the evidence. The principle applies equally to the author and to the institutions he criticises.

Ephesians 4:25 calls for the putting away of falsehood and the speaking of truth. For a witness who believes his work has a spiritual purpose, this is an ethical obligation. A quotation must remain faithful to its source. A claim must be attributed to the correct person. An allegation must not be disguised as a finding. A source must be linked accurately. A conclusion must not exceed the evidence supporting it. If the author has not inspected the complete contents of a document, he must not claim to have conducted a full forensic examination of it. If a page is only a catalogue, it must not be cited as though it were the underlying decision. If a search fails to retrieve a source, that failure must not be presented as evidence that the source is being concealed.

In Matthew 5, Jesus speaks of those who hunger for righteousness and those who are persecuted for righteousness’ sake. These verses have long sustained Christians who believe that fidelity to moral conviction can carry a personal cost. They can provide language for continuing to testify despite adversity. They do not establish that every adverse experience is persecution, or that every person who opposes a claim does so for an improper reason. Those are factual questions. A decision against a claimant may be wrong, correct, incomplete, or addressed to a different legal question than the claimant expected. The biblical text can sustain the believer through the inquiry without deciding the inquiry in advance.

Christian justice, as this paper understands it, is not simply a demand that someone else suffer. It is a demand that wrongdoing be addressed truthfully, that responsibility be identified fairly, and that remedies be proportionate. Where wrongdoing is established, accountability may require correction, explanation, apology, compensation, disciplinary action or another remedy

justified by the facts and the law. Where wrongdoing is not established, the correct response is not to manufacture certainty. Grace does not require the abandonment of accountability. Accountability does not require the abandonment of humanity.

VIII

The archive as a public act of remembrance

An archive is not identical to the argument constructed from it. The archive contains materials. A register helps locate them. A chronology arranges them. An analysis explains relationships. A conclusion makes a claim about what those relationships mean. Each stage requires its own discipline. A document may establish that an official communication was issued, that a decision-maker recorded a particular conclusion, or that a submission was made on a certain date. Whether the decision was legally correct, whether the underlying evidence was complete, whether another person acted improperly, or whether a broader pattern exists, requires additional analysis. Provenance is the history of a document’s origin, custody and transmission. The same words can have different evidentiary significance depending on whether they appear in an original official decision, a party’s submission, a later quotation or an author’s commentary. A forensic reading asks who created the document, which office issued it, when it was created or received, whether the file is an original, a scan, an export or a transcription, whether it carries a reference number, whether attachments are mentioned, whether a later version exists, and whether the text is complete. A filename can provide clues. It is not a substitute for inspection. A repository path helps locate a file. It does not establish authenticity by itself.

One identifiable file in the repository is the McLean and Comcare decision associated with the name ART-2021-7478, dated 12 July 2023. The repository

material previously identified for that file records the SHA-256 value fe78c73aec3be796c36505bfc0da91a41692460045a95b8ac461c4e0f0247d8e. A cryptographic hash is a digital fingerprint of a file’s bytes. If a later copy produces the same value, that is strong evidence the two files are byte-for- byte identical, assuming the comparison was performed correctly. The hash does not establish that the document is genuine merely because it has a fingerprint, and it does not establish that every statement in the document is accurate. Authenticity, integrity, provenance and truth are related. They are not interchangeable.

The filename places the item in an Administrative Appeals Tribunal-related context involving McLean and Comcare. That statement must not be expanded beyond what the actual document supports. A full forensic reading would establish the issuing body, the precise case reference, the statutory provision, the decision-maker’s findings, the evidence discussed, the parties’ submissions and the legal effect. It would establish whether the file is the full decision or another kind of record. A decision about one statutory question does not automatically resolve every related factual dispute. A decision that does not discuss an allegation cannot automatically be treated as having investigated and rejected it. Conversely, disagreement with a decision does not establish that the decision-maker acted improperly.

A public document can remain technically available while being difficult to use. A PDF may be linked but not searchable. A catalogue may contain an entry without the full text. Scanned pages may require optical recognition. None of these conditions, by itself, establishes deliberate concealment. They may result from technical limits, incomplete metadata, broken links or ordinary error. Determining intent requires separate evidence. Nevertheless, poor accessibility has consequences. A person who cannot search a large collection must inspect documents individually. Access to evidence is not the same as proof. It is a condition that makes proof easier to investigate. A public archive can function as counter-memory: a preserved account that allows readers to compare a person’s testimony with other records and institutional narratives. Counter-memory is not automatically correct. A

personal archive may contain accurate official documents, incomplete correspondence, subjective interpretations and unverified allegations. An official record can be accurate about the decision it records while remaining limited in scope. Neither category should be accepted or dismissed merely because of its origin. The method is comparison. The archive should be a place where evidence can challenge the author’s interpretation as well as support it.

XVII

Five categories, so the paper can be

answered

A serious reading of this corpus should keep five categories separate, and should apply them to every strong sentence above.

A documented fact is a proposition directly established by a reliable record, inside that record’s proper scope. An official record is a document issued or maintained by an identifiable institution. Its status as an official record does not mean every interpretation of it is correct. An author allegation is a claim made by Barrandodger or another writer that has not necessarily been independently established. An inference is a conclusion drawn from one or more facts. Its strength depends on the quality of the evidence and on the plausibility of other explanations. An unverified proposition is a claim for which the available material has not yet supplied adequate corroboration. Applied here: the existence of the named repository, and of particular files inside it, can be reported as a feature of the published archive. The hash of a file can be reported as a fingerprint of that file. The legal conclusions inside the Comcare decision can be described only from the decision’s text. The unspoken mandate is an inference offered by an assisted extraction, not a located order. The assassination allegation is testimony. The chosen-witness declaration is faith. The gospel volumes are literary theology. The Able Point complaint is a complaint. The paradox of survival is a theory of motive. None of these sentences becomes another kind of sentence because they share a binding.

Corroboration occurs when relevant evidence from another source supports a proposition. Two web pages that repeat the same author’s statement are not two independent confirmations. A later summary that copies an earlier document does not independently verify the underlying event. A site rebuilt from a preservation copy is a finding aid, not a second witness. The church site is ministry. Ministry is allowed to be ministry. It is not allowed to pretend it is a second government file.
