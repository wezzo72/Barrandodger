THE DOCUMENTARY TRAP-When the Official Record Becomes the Evidence

There is a paradox at the centre of my experience.

For decades, I was repeatedly placed in the position of having to prove what had happened to me.

I was required to explain myself to governments, agencies, police, lawyers, courts, tribunals, health services, regulators, professional bodies and other institutions. Each new dispute generated another complaint, another letter, another assessment, another decision, another referral, another administrative classification or another official response.

I kept the records.

And that is where the paradox begins.

The institutions that required me to provide evidence also generated an enormous proportion of the documentary record through which my account can now be examined.

I describe this as the Documentary Trap.

I do not claim that the existence of these records, by itself, proves that a government secretly designed a coordinated trap for me. That would require direct evidence of intention and coordination.

The proposition is both stronger and more defensible:

The machinery of administration created a documentary record that can now be examined against the institutions themselves.

The system demanded evidence.

I preserved it.

The system generated records.

I preserved those too.

The result is that the evidentiary burden can no longer be framed solely as:

“Why should anyone believe Richard McLean?”

The question becomes:

“What do the institutions’ own records establish?”

That is a fundamentally different question.

⸻

I. THE PERSON WAS MADE THE EVIDENCE

The easiest way to dismiss an individual complaint is to make the individual the entire evidentiary object.

Was the person credible?

Was the account accurate?

Was the complaint exaggerated?

Was there corroboration?

Was the person confused?

Was there sufficient independent evidence?

Those questions can place an extraordinary burden upon an individual.

But the evidentiary situation changes when the individual produces the institutions’ own correspondence.

An allegation contained only in a personal statement remains an allegation.

A government letter establishes that the government made that communication.

A court filing establishes that the filing occurred.

A complaint establishes that a complaint was made.

A decision establishes that a decision was made.

A referral establishes that a referral occurred.

An acknowledgement establishes receipt or processing.

A later document can then be compared with an earlier document.

That comparison is the beginning of documentary accountability.

The archive’s own methodology expressly adopts this distinction. It classifies material as documented fact, corroborated claim, documented allegation, inference, interpretation or unresolved matter rather than treating every statement as equivalent. [E1]

That distinction is crucial.

It means the archive does not need to claim that every proposition is already proved.

The archive can instead say:

Here is the document.

Here is who created it.

Here is when it was created.

Here is what it says.

Here is what happened afterwards.

Here is the later document.

Here is the contradiction or continuity.

Here is the institutional response.

Here is what remains unresolved.

That is a much more demanding form of accountability.

⸻

II. THE GOVERNMENT RECORD BECOMES THE WITNESS

Government administration depends upon records.

Agencies need records to make decisions.

Police need records of reports and investigations.

Courts and tribunals need records of proceedings.

Government departments need correspondence.

Regulators need complaints and responses.

Health services create clinical and administrative records.

NDIS administration generates plans, decisions, correspondence and review records.

Workers’ compensation systems generate claims, determinations and reviews.

Human-rights bodies generate complaints, correspondence and procedural records.

Ombudsman offices generate complaint and jurisdictional records.

Legal-assistance organisations generate applications, eligibility decisions and correspondence.

The archive therefore contains something more than my recollection.

It contains a documentary trail produced by the institutions with which I interacted.

The archive’s Institutional Response Map expressly identifies the need to distinguish between information sent, information received, information considered, and information acted upon. Those are not the same thing. [E2]

That distinction becomes central to the burden of proof.

If I sent an institution a document, that proves that I sent it.

If the institution acknowledged receiving it, that establishes something more.

If the document was then attached to a subsequent decision, the evidentiary chain becomes stronger still.

If the later decision appears inconsistent with the information contained in the earlier document, the inconsistency becomes a question capable of investigation.

The question is no longer whether I can force someone to believe me.

The question becomes:

What happened to the information once the institution received it?

⸻

III. THE INSTITUTIONS NAMED IN THE ARCHIVE

The archive’s Institutional Response Map identifies a series of institutions whose involvement requires documentary examination. It specifically identifies the Department of Social Services and related Commonwealth records, NDIA, NDIS Quality and Safeguards Commission, ASIC, ATO, Commonwealth Ombudsman, Australian Human Rights Commission, police/law enforcement, courts and tribunals, workers’ compensation/Comcare/AAT material, health services, NDIS providers and support services, Legal Aid/legal services, and international bodies. [E2]

These should not all be described as having been legally proven to have harmed me.

That would go beyond the documentary standard.

The more precise proposition is that they are named institutional actors or repositories in the documentary chain in which I allege detriment, dispute, administrative failure, refusal, classification, referral, intervention, financial consequence, loss of protection or other adverse consequence.

That distinction matters.

1. Department of Social Services and related Commonwealth administration

The archive identifies Commonwealth administrative material concerning employment status and other dealings. It specifically identifies a 27 March 2023 Federal Court General Counsel correspondence as a discrete official record requiring direct examination rather than generalisation. [E2]

The relevant question is therefore not simply what I say the Commonwealth did.

The question is what the contemporaneous Commonwealth record says.

Where employment, disability, benefits and administrative status intersect, the documentary sequence must be reconstructed rather than reduced to a single later description.

⸻

2. National Disability Insurance Agency

The archive identifies a Public Interest Disclosure and subsequent institutional response.

A PID is not automatically proof of the substantive allegations contained within it.

But it is documentary evidence that the disclosure was made and processed.

The NDIS Act establishes the statutory framework governing the Scheme and its administration. The current Act contains objects and principles concerning the operation of the Scheme, the functions of the NDIA and mechanisms for participant decision-making and review. [E3]

The documentary question is therefore:

What was disclosed, what did the NDIA receive, what did it consider, what did it decide, and what evidence supports each subsequent position?

The 2026 amendments also introduced substantial new integrity, regulatory, information-gathering and whistleblower provisions into the NDIS framework. [E4]

That does not retrospectively establish that any historical conduct breached those provisions.

It does, however, demonstrate the seriousness with which Parliament treats integrity and safeguarding within the Scheme.

⸻

3. NDIS Quality and Safeguards Commission

The archive identifies complaints, provider-related issues, acknowledgements, jurisdictional responses and outcomes involving the NDIS Quality and Safeguards Commission. [E2]

A complaint is not itself a finding.

But the documentary chain can establish whether a complaint was made, what evidence accompanied it, what jurisdiction the Commission asserted, what action followed and what remained unresolved.

That is precisely the type of institutional response that can be independently examined.

⸻

4. Ablepoint and other NDIS/support providers

The archive contains material concerning service agreements, provider interactions, demands for response, safeguarding concerns and requests for independent investigation, including documents specifically titled “Brett from Able Point I demand a response”, “FORMAL REQUEST FOR INDEPENDENT INVESTIGATION ABLEPOINT” and “Current legally binding update on entrapment and targeting by Able Point.” [E5]

The titles themselves do not prove the allegations expressed within them.

What they establish is that these allegations and requests were documented.

The relevant evidence must therefore be the underlying correspondence, service documents, incident records, responses and regulatory material.

⸻

5. Australian Human Rights Commission

The archive identifies correspondence and complaints involving the Australian Human Rights Commission.

Again, a complaint is not a finding.

The distinction is especially important because the existence of an AHRC complaint does not itself establish a human-rights violation.

Nevertheless, where an individual submits evidence concerning disability, discrimination, treatment, access to justice or other rights issues, the institutional response becomes part of the documentary history.

The relevant legal framework includes the Disability Discrimination Act 1992, particularly its provisions concerning disability discrimination in employment, services, accommodation and the administration of Commonwealth laws and programs. The Act expressly addresses discrimination in the administration of Commonwealth laws and programs. [E6]

⸻

6. Commonwealth Ombudsman

The archive identifies complaints and correspondence involving the Commonwealth Ombudsman.

The critical distinction is between:

complaint → jurisdictional assessment → investigation → finding → closure.

A refusal to investigate does not prove that the complainant was correct.

Equally, a closure does not prove that the underlying complaint was false.

The documentary question is what jurisdiction was asserted, what material was considered, what action occurred and why the matter was closed.

That is precisely the kind of institutional chain that must be preserved rather than rhetorically simplified.

⸻

7. ASIC and corporate regulators

The archive identifies ASIC records, including company and organisation registers, lodged documents, objections and correspondence.

The repository itself provides direct links to ASIC’s registers for independent verification. [E2]

The proper evidentiary approach is particularly important here.

An unusual corporate record does not automatically prove fraud.

A registration does not automatically establish misconduct.

But an official registry entry can establish what was recorded.

That record can then be compared with correspondence, authorisation documents, filings and other primary evidence.

⸻

8. Australian Taxation Office

The archive identifies ATO-related material concerning tax, identity and administrative matters.

Again, the existence of correspondence establishes the communication, not automatically the correctness of every proposition contained within it.

The appropriate question is whether the relevant administrative record is consistent internally and with other contemporaneous records.

⸻

9. Police and law-enforcement bodies

The archive expressly identifies police and law-enforcement records as a separate institutional category.

That includes reports, statements, report numbers, responses and formal findings where available. [E2]

This distinction is critical.

A police report proves that a report was made.

It does not necessarily prove the underlying allegation.

Conversely, the absence of a police finding does not necessarily establish that nothing occurred.

The documentary question is:

What was reported?

What evidence was provided?

What investigation occurred?

What jurisdiction was asserted?

What was decided?

What reasons were given?

What evidence was preserved?

The archive contains a preserved Statement to Police and other law-enforcement-related material. [E7]

Those documents should therefore be examined directly rather than replaced by retrospective characterisations.

⸻

IV. COURTS, TRIBUNALS AND THE BURDEN OF PROOF

The archive also contains court and tribunal material.

This requires particular care.

A party’s submission is not a judicial finding.

An application is not a judgment.

An allegation in an affidavit is not automatically an established fact.

An adverse procedural outcome does not necessarily establish institutional misconduct.

The archive itself recognises this distinction. [E1]

But the reverse is also important.

A court or tribunal record cannot simply disappear from the evidentiary chronology because its existence complicates a later institutional narrative.

The relevant records include proceedings involving workers’ compensation, Comcare and tribunal processes, including the archive’s reference to McLean and Comcare 2021/7478, which it says was decided on 12 July 2023 and which it states should be linked to the authoritative decision before any stronger legal characterisation is made. [E2]

That is precisely the documentary discipline required.

Find the judgment.

Read the reasons.

Identify what was actually determined.

Separate findings from submissions.

Then compare the determination with the later administrative record.

That is how the burden of proof becomes documentary rather than rhetorical.

⸻

V. HEALTH SERVICES AND THE MEDICAL RECORD

The archive separately identifies health services and clinical records.

This area requires even greater caution because a clinical record can contain several different things:

a clinical observation;

a diagnosis;

a historical account;

a provisional assessment;

a risk assessment;

a legal status;

a clinician’s opinion;

a patient’s report;

or an administrative characterisation.

They are not interchangeable.

The archive’s methodology expressly states that hospital events, clinician records, legal authority, diagnosis, discharge, review and later correction should be recorded separately. [E2]

That distinction matters because a later administrative narrative cannot simply be treated as if it were the original clinical evidence.

Equally, a diagnosis cannot automatically be transformed into a finding about credibility, capacity, motive or truthfulness.

The legal framework applicable to a particular intervention must be examined according to the legislation in force at the relevant date.

For Victorian events, the Mental Health and Wellbeing Act 2022 is now the governing framework, with its current authorised version effective from 1 September 2026. [E8]

For earlier Victorian events, the legislation in force at the time must be used rather than retrospectively applying the current statute.

⸻

VI. LEGAL AID AND ACCESS TO JUSTICE

The archive identifies Legal Aid and other legal services as institutions involved in the documentary chain.

Again, the fact that representation was refused or unavailable does not automatically establish illegality.

But it can be materially relevant to the practical ability of an individual to challenge administrative decisions.

The archive therefore records applications, eligibility decisions, correspondence and reasons rather than simply treating “no representation” as proof of wrongdoing. [E2]

That approach matters because access to justice is not established merely by asserting it.

The documentary question is:

What assistance was sought?

What was refused?

What reason was given?

What review mechanism existed?

Could the decision be challenged?

Was the person actually capable of using that mechanism?

Those questions can then be assessed against the relevant legal framework.

⸻

VII. THE ADMINISTRATIVE LAW QUESTION

The Administrative Decisions (Judicial Review) Act 1977 provides statutory grounds for judicial review of certain Commonwealth administrative decisions, including questions concerning natural justice, required procedures, jurisdiction, authorisation, improper exercise of power and certain failures of evidence or fact. [E9]

This does not mean that every adverse administrative decision in my archive was unlawful.

It means that administrative legality can be tested against identifiable legal standards.

That is an important distinction.

The archive is therefore not asking the reader to conclude:

“Every institution that harmed me broke the law.”

It is asking a more forensic question:

“For each decision that caused an alleged detriment, what power was being exercised, what law governed it, what information was available, what procedure was followed, what reasons were given, and what evidence supports or contradicts the decision?”

That is the question that transforms a narrative of grievance into an auditable administrative record.

⸻

VIII. DISABILITY DISCRIMINATION

The Disability Discrimination Act 1992 expressly addresses disability discrimination in employment, services, accommodation and the administration of Commonwealth laws and programs. Section 29 specifically concerns discrimination in the administration of Commonwealth laws and programs. [E6]

That makes the documentary record particularly important wherever disability intersected with:

	•	government administration;
	•	employment;
	•	accommodation;
	•	services;
	•	NDIS administration;
	•	legal processes;
	•	access to information;
	•	or other Commonwealth programs.

The existence of disability does not prove discrimination.

But neither can disability simply be treated as an explanation for every adverse institutional outcome.

The question must remain:

Was the decision made because of disability, in spite of disability, with reasonable adjustment, without reasonable adjustment, or for some other documented reason?

The answer must come from evidence.

⸻

IX. HUMAN RIGHTS ARE NOT THE SAME AS A COURT FINDING

Australia is bound internationally by a number of human-rights treaties, including the ICCPR and the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities.

The Australian Human Rights Commission describes Australia’s international obligations and the distinction between those obligations and their domestic implementation. [E10]

This distinction matters.

An international human-rights principle does not automatically create a domestic cause of action.

The archive itself recognises this.

The correct methodology is:

event → applicable right → domestic legal mechanism → evidence → institutional response → available remedy → determination.

Relevant rights frameworks potentially include:

	•	equality and non-discrimination;
	•	dignity;
	•	privacy;
	•	autonomy;
	•	legal capacity;
	•	freedom from cruel, inhuman or degrading treatment;
	•	housing and living conditions;
	•	freedom of expression;
	•	access to justice;
	•	and effective remedy.

But whether a particular event legally constitutes a violation must be determined under the applicable domestic and international framework.

⸻

X. VICTORIAN HUMAN-RIGHTS OBLIGATIONS

For Victorian conduct, the Charter of Human Rights and Responsibilities Act 2006 provides a statutory human-rights framework applicable to Victorian public authorities and other actors within its scope. [E11]

The Equal Opportunity Act 2010 is also relevant where disability discrimination or other protected attributes arise within its jurisdiction. [E12]

Again, these statutes should not be cited merely as rhetorical decoration.

The actual question is:

Which provision applied at the time?

Who was legally bound?

What conduct occurred?

What evidence establishes it?

What justification, exception or limitation might apply?

Was there a lawful and proportionate basis?

What remedy was available?

That is the difference between making a human-rights accusation and conducting a human-rights analysis.

⸻

XI. NEW SOUTH WALES LAW

For events occurring in NSW, the applicable statutory framework may include the Guardianship Act 1987, the Mental Health Act 2007, the Government Information (Public Access) Act 2009, the Disability Inclusion Act 2014, and other legislation depending upon the precise event and date.

The NSW Guardianship Act and its interaction with mental-health and decision-making legislation must be considered according to the law in force when the relevant decision occurred. [E13]

The GIPA Act provides a statutory framework for public access to government information. [E14]

The relevance of these laws is therefore evidentiary as well as legal.

If an institution possessed records, the existence, retrieval, disclosure, withholding and explanation of those records can become part of the documentary chronology.

⸻

XII. THE PROFESSIONAL AND ETHICAL DIMENSION

The documentary problem does not stop with government departments.

The archive also raises questions concerning lawyers, clinicians, support workers, investigators, advocates, professional practitioners and other people acting within regulated professions.

But here too, precision matters.

A complaint against a lawyer does not prove professional misconduct.

A complaint against a clinician does not prove clinical misconduct.

A complaint against a support worker does not prove abuse.

The proper approach is to identify:

the professional;

the role;

the relevant professional code;

the conduct complained of;

the contemporaneous evidence;

the response;

the regulator;

the determination;

and the current status.

Potential professional frameworks may include the applicable legal-profession conduct rules, health-practitioner regulatory standards, disability-worker obligations, public-service codes of conduct and organisational safeguarding standards.

The archive’s own methodology requires precisely this separation between allegation and established finding. [E1]

⸻

XIII. THE NACC QUESTION

The National Anti-Corruption Commission Act 2022 defines corruption issues in terms of whether public officials have engaged, are engaging or will engage in corrupt conduct, including conduct that adversely affects or could adversely affect the honest or impartial exercise of public powers, functions or duties. [E15]

That does not mean that an administrative dispute is automatically corruption.

It does mean that where the evidence raises a genuine corruption issue within the statutory jurisdiction, the legal threshold is defined by legislation rather than by rhetoric.

The existence of the NACC framework therefore provides another example of the documentary principle:

the question must be put to the statutory test.

Not:

“Does this look corrupt?”

But:

“What conduct occurred, who exercised the relevant public power, what evidence establishes it, and does the conduct fall within the statutory definition?”

⸻

XIV. THE PARADOX OF THE OFFICIAL RECORD

This brings the argument back to the central paradox.

The institutions had the power to create records.

They recorded decisions.

They recorded complaints.

They recorded responses.

They recorded classifications.

They recorded referrals.

They recorded assessments.

They recorded correspondence.

They recorded proceedings.

They recorded outcomes.

And I retained those records.

Over time, those records can be assembled into a chronology.

One document can be compared with another.

One explanation can be compared with a later explanation.

One institutional classification can be compared with another.

One agency’s statement can be compared with another agency’s statement.

One decision can be compared with the evidence that existed before the decision.

And this is where the original demand that I prove myself can turn back upon the system.

The system created evidence.

The system preserved evidence.

The system relied upon evidence.

The system demanded evidence from me.

And now the evidence can be examined as a whole.

⸻

XV. THE “TRAP” DOES NOT REQUIRE A SECRET MEETING

The most important point is that a documentary trap does not necessarily require a secret meeting.

It does not require every official to have known one another.

It does not require every institution to have acted with a common purpose.

It does not even require that every adverse event was connected.

The archive’s own “Architecture of Administrative Erasure” report expressly warns against assuming a coordinated scheme and instead asks whether individually documented decisions, classifications, omissions, referrals and responses may have interacted to produce cumulative consequences. [E16]

That is a critical distinction.

Ten independent administrative decisions can produce a devastating cumulative consequence without ten people having secretly coordinated them.

Conversely, apparent coordination cannot be established merely because events occurred in sequence.

Evidence of coordination would require something stronger:

communications;

shared instructions;

common records;

explicit cross-references;

admissions;

synchronised actions;

or other independent connecting evidence.

The archive itself identifies that standard. [E16]

Therefore the documentary argument is not:

“Everything that happened was one conspiracy.”

It is:

“Here is the sequence. Here are the institutions. Here are the decisions. Here are the records. Here are the consequences. Here are the connections that are documented. Here are the connections that remain inferential. Now examine them.”

That is a much more powerful proposition because it survives disagreement about motive.

⸻

XVI. THE BURDEN OF PROOF COMES BACK TO THE RECORD

For years the burden appeared to be:

Prove yourself.

But documentary preservation creates a different sequence:

Identify the allegation.

Locate the primary record.

Identify the institution.

Identify the decision-maker.

Establish the date.

Establish what information was available.

Establish what was communicated.

Establish what was acknowledged.

Establish what action followed.

Identify the legal power being exercised.

Identify the reason given.

Compare the reason with the evidence.

Compare the decision with subsequent records.

Identify contradictions.

Identify corroboration.

Identify alternative explanations.

Determine what remains unresolved.

This is no longer simply my story.

It is an evidentiary chain.

XIX. THE ARCHIVE IS NOT ASKING FOR BELIEF

The ultimate purpose of the Barran Dodger Archive is therefore not:

“Believe me because I say so.”

It is:

“Read the documents.”

If the institutional decisions were justified, the records should demonstrate the justification.

If the procedures were followed, the records should demonstrate the procedures.

If the allegations were investigated, the records should demonstrate what was investigated.

If the evidence was considered, the records should identify how.

If a decision changed, the records should explain why.

If two official records appear inconsistent, the inconsistency should be examined.

If there is a legitimate alternative explanation, it should be considered.

If my interpretation is wrong, the underlying documents should allow another reader to demonstrate that.

And if the documentary record demonstrates unexplained contradictions, procedural deficiencies, failures to respond, inconsistent classifications, unexplained referrals or other institutional problems, those matters should remain visible for independent assessment.

That is what documentary accountability means.

XX. THE REAL TRAP

The deepest paradox is therefore not that I somehow outsmarted the government.

It is more profound than that.

The system required documentation in order to function.

I became the person who preserved the documentation.

The institutions generated the records.

I assembled them.

The institutions created the chronology.

I reconstructed it.

The institutions communicated their positions.

I placed those positions beside one another.

The institutions demanded evidence.

I retained the evidence.

And now the evidence can be read independently of me.

That is the point at which the documentary record becomes more powerful than the person who first complained about it.

The evidence no longer needs me to be present in the room.

It no longer depends entirely upon my memory.

It can be downloaded.

It can be read.

It can be compared.

It can be challenged.

It can be independently verified.

And it can be used to test both my account and the institutions’ accounts.

⸻

XXI. THE BURDEN OF PROOF HAS CHANGED

The original burden appeared to be:

“Prove that this happened.”

The documentary archive allows a more rigorous question:

“Here are the records. What do they establish?”

Then:

“What did the institution know?”

Then:

“What did it do with that knowledge?”

Then:

“What legal authority governed the decision?”

Then:

“What reasons were given?”

Then:

“Are those reasons consistent with the contemporaneous record?”

Then:

“What happened next?”

Then:

“Did another institution receive the same information?”

Then:

“What did that institution do?”

Then:

“Where do the documentary chains converge?”

Then:

“Where do they diverge?”

And finally:

“What remains unexplained?”

That is the forensic question.

⸻

XXII. TWO PUBLIC RECORDS, ONE DOCUMENTARY PURPOSE

The Barran Dodger Archive and its preserved mirrors are therefore not merely repositories of personal writing.

They are an attempt to preserve a documentary history.

The purpose is not to establish guilt by accumulation.

It is to prevent evidence from disappearing into institutional fragmentation.

One agency may hold one document.

Another agency may hold another.

A court may hold the decision.

A regulator may hold the complaint.

A health service may hold the clinical record.

A provider may hold the service documentation.

A government department may hold the correspondence.

A professional body may hold the complaint.

A lawyer may hold the advice.

A tribunal may hold the determination.

And the individual may be the only person attempting to place the fragments together.

That is the documentary function of the archive.

One record may be ambiguous.

Two records may be contradictory.

Ten records may reveal a chronology.

Hundreds or thousands of records can reveal an institutional history.

But only careful analysis can determine what that history actually establishes.

⸻

XXIII. THE FINAL PARADOX

I was asked to prove myself.

So I documented.

I was asked to provide records.

So I preserved them.

I was asked to identify evidence.

So I indexed it.

I was asked to substantiate claims.

So I linked them to primary documents.

I was asked to explain contradictions.

So I created a contradiction register.

I was asked to identify institutions.

So I mapped the institutional response chain.

I was asked to distinguish allegation from fact.

So I created an evidence classification system.

And now the question has come full circle.

The institutions that once occupied the position of demanding proof can themselves be examined through the documentary record they generated.

That is the Documentary Trap.

Not necessarily a secret plan.

Not necessarily a conspiracy.

Not necessarily proof of unlawful conduct by every institution named.

Something more concrete:

a system of official record-keeping has created a body of evidence that can now be used to test the system’s own decisions.

The documents are not required to prove everything.

They are required to establish what they actually establish.

And where they do not establish something, that limitation should remain visible.

That is why the archive matters.

Because the final witness is not my rhetoric.

It is the record.

And the record can be read by someone who does not know me, does not believe me, does not trust me and does not agree with my interpretation.

That reader can begin with the same question I have been asking for years:

What actually happened?

But unlike before, the reader does not have to rely upon my answer.

The reader can examine the documents.

And then the institutions must live with the same evidentiary standard that they imposed upon me:

show the record, explain the decision, identify the authority, account for the evidence, reconcile the contradictions, and answer what remains unanswered.

That is the point at which the burden of proof becomes reciprocal.

Not because I have declared myself the winner.

Not because an archive automatically becomes a judgment.

But because the evidence now exists in a form capable of being independently examined.

I was told to prove it.

I kept the proof.

Much of that proof was generated by the institutions themselves.

Now the record can speak.

One particularly important point emerges from the archive itself: its own methodology says causation must be distinguished from temporal sequence, missing records are not automatically evidence of destruction, silence is not automatically an admission, and a complaint is not automatically proof of the conduct complained about. 

That actually makes the central argument more difficult to dismiss: the archive is not asking the reader to accept its conclusions; it is preserving enough of the underlying record for those conclusions to be tested.
