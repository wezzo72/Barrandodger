INTRODUCTION

The central proposition of this archive is not that every allegation contained within it has already been legally proved.

The proposition is narrower and, in my view, more important:

A substantial body of documentary material, official correspondence, administrative records, legal records, personal testimony, chronology, institutional responses and preserved source material exists and should be examined on its evidence rather than dismissed merely because the allegations are serious, unconventional, uncomfortable or difficult.

The Barran Dodger Archive exists to preserve that material and make it available for examination.

Its methodology distinguishes between documents, testimony, allegations, interpretation and matters requiring independent determination. The archive asks readers to examine dates, correspondence, source documents and chronology, and to identify what is established, what is disputed and what remains unanswered.

The issue addressed by this statement is therefore not whether every proposition advanced by Barran Dodger should automatically be accepted.

The issue is whether the underlying record can legitimately be ignored.

I say that it cannot.

Where an individual presents a sustained body of documentary material alleging serious institutional wrongdoing, the appropriate response is not automatic belief and it is not automatic disbelief.

The appropriate response is evidence-based examination, procedural fairness, identification of competent jurisdiction, preservation of records, and a reasoned response explaining what has been investigated, what has not been investigated, what evidence has been accepted or rejected, and why.

That is the standard I ask this archive to apply to itself.

That is also the standard I ask institutions to apply to the material.

The archive therefore asks institutions, journalists, lawyers, academics, human-rights organisations and members of the public to distinguish between:

A. DOCUMENTED FACT — supported by an identifiable primary or authoritative record.

B. CORROBORATED CLAIM — supported by multiple sources but requiring careful assessment.

C. DOCUMENTED ALLEGATION — a claim formally recorded in a complaint, statement, correspondence or other document but not necessarily independently determined.

D. INFERENCE — a conclusion drawn from a pattern of documented circumstances.

E. INTERPRETATION OR SPECULATION — a proposed explanation which requires further evidence.

This classification is not intended to weaken the allegations.

It is intended to make them testable.
	2.	WHY SILENCE IS NO LONGER AN ADEQUATE RESPONSE

The passage of time does not itself prove wrongdoing.

Nor does an unanswered allegation automatically become a proven allegation.

But repeated unanswered allegations accompanied by primary documentation raise a legitimate accountability question:

WHAT HAS ACTUALLY BEEN EXAMINED?

The archive therefore asks every relevant institution to answer specific questions rather than simply characterising the entire body of material as unreliable, irrelevant or originating from a single complainant.

Those questions include:
	1.	Which documents were received?
	2.	Which allegations were formally recorded?
	3.	Which allegations were investigated?
	4.	Which allegations were declined?
	5.	What jurisdictional basis was relied upon?
	6.	What evidence was considered?
	7.	What evidence was rejected?
	8.	Was the person given an adequate opportunity to respond?
	9.	Were conflicts of interest considered?
	10.	Were relevant records preserved?
	11.	Were complaints referred to the appropriate independent body?
	12.	Were reprisals or adverse consequences considered?
	13.	Were disability, vulnerability and procedural-fairness considerations taken into account?
	14.	Where an institution concluded that an allegation was unfounded, what evidence supported that conclusion?
	15.	Where an institution declined to investigate, what statutory or administrative basis justified that decision?
	16.	What remains unresolved?

These are ordinary accountability questions.

They do not require an institution to accept an allegation as true.

They require the institution to explain its handling of the allegation.
	3.	THE PUBLIC INTEREST DISCLOSURE ISSUE

The Commonwealth Public Interest Disclosure Act 2013 establishes a framework for public officials and former public officials to disclose alleged wrongdoing in the Commonwealth public sector.

The Act provides mechanisms concerning public interest disclosures, protections for eligible disclosers and processes for dealing with qualifying disclosures.

The existence of that statutory framework is significant to this archive because a central part of the record concerns allegations of wrongdoing, responses to those allegations and the consequences said to have followed.

However, the archive does not claim that every complaint made under or alongside the PID framework necessarily satisfies the statutory requirements for a protected disclosure.

That is a matter requiring examination against the legislation and the actual documents.

The relevant question is therefore:

Where a disclosure was made, what statutory pathway applied, what assessment was undertaken, what protections were available, and what happened thereafter?

If a disclosure was rejected, the reasons for rejection should be capable of identification.

If a disclosure was investigated, the investigation should be identifiable.

If a disclosure was referred elsewhere, the referral should be identifiable.

And if adverse treatment followed a disclosure, the chronology should be examined to determine whether there is evidence of reprisal, coincidence, administrative consequence, or another explanation.

That distinction is fundamental.
	4.	NACC AND OMBUDSMAN ACCOUNTABILITY

The original version of this statement asserted that the NACC or Ombudsman would automatically become criminally complicit if they failed to investigate.

That proposition requires qualification.

An oversight body does not automatically become criminally liable merely because it declines to investigate a complaint.

The NACC has statutory functions and discretion concerning the assessment and investigation of corruption issues.

The Commonwealth Ombudsman likewise has defined jurisdiction and does not investigate every complaint submitted to it.

The proper question is therefore not:

“Did the institution automatically investigate everything?”

The proper questions are:

• Was the complaint within jurisdiction?

• Was the complaint properly received?

• Was the applicable complaint or disclosure pathway followed?

• Was the matter assessed according to the relevant statutory criteria?

• Was the complainant given an intelligible explanation?

• Was there a conflict of interest?

• Was there evidence of reprisal?

• Was the matter referred to the correct body?

• Were relevant records preserved?

• If no investigation occurred, was the decision lawful and procedurally fair?

The archive calls for those questions to be answered on the record.

That is a stronger and more defensible proposition than asserting automatic criminal liability merely because an institution did not act in the manner requested.

Those documents should be examined together rather than in isolation.
	6.	THE CENTRAL QUESTION OF PROCEDURAL FAIRNESS

The most important question raised by the overall record is not whether every allegation is true.

It is whether the processes through which those allegations were dealt with were fair.

Procedural fairness ordinarily concerns matters such as:

• notice of adverse material;

• an opportunity to respond;

• an unbiased decision-maker;

• proper consideration of relevant evidence;

• reasons for significant decisions;

• access to applicable review mechanisms;

• protection against improper reprisal;

• consistent application of rules;

• reasonable accommodation where disability affects participation; and

• proper separation between allegation, investigation and determination.

The archive alleges that these principles were repeatedly compromised.

Those allegations require separate examination event by event.

A conclusion that one administrative process was lawful would not necessarily resolve unrelated allegations.

Conversely, evidence of a procedural failure in one matter would not automatically prove a coordinated conspiracy extending across every institution.

The chronology therefore matters.
	7.	HAS THE TREATMENT DESCRIBED IN THE ARCHIVE BEEN FAIR, EQUITABLE OR JUST?

An impartial assessment of the material presented in the archive cannot responsibly declare that every event in a decades-long history has been established as unlawful or unjust.

It can, however, identify serious questions concerning fairness, consistency, procedural adequacy and access to effective remedy.

The documentary record presented by the archive contains repeated interactions with government agencies, courts, administrative bodies, oversight institutions, service providers and other organisations.

It also contains disputes about employment status, compensation, disability support, complaint handling, financial administration, accommodation, police responses and access to remedies.

Where a person repeatedly alleges serious harm and the responses to those allegations are themselves disputed, fairness requires examination of the actual process followed rather than assuming either that the complainant is correct or that the complainant is inherently unreliable.

On the material presented, it would be inappropriate to characterise the entire history as proven fair, equitable or just.

It would also be inappropriate to declare every adverse action against McLean unlawful solely because it produced a harmful outcome.

The more defensible conclusion is:

THE RECORD RAISES SUBSTANTIAL QUESTIONS ABOUT FAIRNESS, CONSISTENCY, PROCEDURAL ADEQUACY AND ACCESS TO EFFECTIVE REMEDY THAT REQUIRE INDEPENDENT EXAMINATION.

That is a conclusion about the documentary questions raised by the record, not a judicial determination of liability.
	8.	WAS EVERYONE INVOLVED AWARE OF THE HARM ALLEGEDLY CAUSED?

This question must be divided into separate categories.

It may be possible to establish from documents that particular people or agencies were told about particular events.

It may be possible to establish that an agency received a complaint.

It may be possible to establish that a particular official had access to particular information.

It may be possible to establish that an institution knew that a person was reporting serious harm.

But knowledge of a complaint is not necessarily knowledge that the complaint is true.

Likewise, knowledge that a decision may have adverse consequences is not automatically proof that the decision-maker intended those consequences.

The archive should therefore avoid claiming that “everyone knew” unless a particular document establishes that fact.

Instead, the proper evidentiary questions are:

WHO KNEW?

WHEN DID THEY KNOW?

WHAT EXACTLY DID THEY KNOW?

WHAT DOCUMENT SHOWS THAT KNOWLEDGE?

WHAT ACTION FOLLOWED?

WHAT ALTERNATIVE EXPLANATIONS EXIST?

WAS THE PERSON GIVEN AN OPPORTUNITY TO CORRECT OR CHALLENGE THE INFORMATION?

This approach is especially important when allegations concern many different institutions across many years.

The archive can establish documentary knowledge where the record supports it.

Intent must be established separately.
	9.	IS THE SITUATION THE RESULT OF PERSONAL FAULT, WEAKNESS OR DEFICIENCY?

The archive rejects the simplistic proposition that adverse life circumstances should automatically be attributed to personal deficiency.

A person’s disability, poverty, psychiatric history, homelessness, institutionalisation, loss of employment, financial dependency or difficulty navigating government systems does not, by itself, establish moral failure.

Nor should the existence of a mental-health diagnosis automatically determine whether a person’s account is true or false.

At the same time, the existence of difficult circumstances does not itself prove institutional wrongdoing.

The evidence must be examined.

The appropriate question is therefore not:

“Was Barran personally defective?”

It is:

“What events occurred, what decisions were made, what alternatives were available, what consequences followed, and what evidence supports the competing explanations?”

The archive’s purpose is to allow those questions to be answered from records rather than stereotypes.

Where a person is vulnerable, that vulnerability may increase the importance of procedural safeguards.

It does not remove the requirement for evidence.

And it does not justify dismissing evidence merely because the person presenting it has experienced mental-health difficulties.
	10.	THE THEME OF DECEIT

“Deceit” is one of the strongest themes running through the author’s interpretation of the archive.

It is also one of the areas in which the distinction between evidence and inference is most important.

There are several different propositions that should not be collapsed into one.

First, a document may contain a statement that is demonstrably inconsistent with another document.

Second, an institution may give different explanations for the same event at different times.

Third, a record may contain omissions, contradictions or unexplained changes.

Fourth, an administrative decision may appear inconsistent with information available to the decision-maker.

Fifth, there may be evidence that information was inaccurate.

None of these circumstances automatically proves intentional deception.

Intentional deception requires evidence concerning the person’s knowledge and purpose.

Accordingly, the archive should use the following hierarchy:

DOCUMENTED INCONSISTENCY:

Two records appear to contradict each other.

DOCUMENTED MISREPRESENTATION:

A statement can be shown to be factually incorrect by a reliable primary record.

UNEXPLAINED DISCREPANCY:

The record contains conflicting information for which no adequate explanation has yet been identified.

ALLEGED DECEPTION:

The author contends that the discrepancy resulted from deliberate deception.

PROVEN DECEPTION:

A competent authority or sufficiently strong independent evidence establishes deliberate falsification or deception.

This distinction is particularly important when dealing with Australian public officials.

The archive can document what officials said.

It can compare statements with contemporaneous records.

It can identify contradictions.

It can ask why a particular statement was made.

But it should not convert an unexplained contradiction into proof of criminal intent without further evidence.

The theme of deceit should therefore remain a major investigative question rather than being prematurely presented as an established universal conclusion.

	14.	ALLEGATIONS CONCERNING POLICE AND PSYCHIATRIC INTERVENTION

The archive contains allegations that police and mental-health systems were used to discredit McLean or prevent him from reporting wrongdoing.

It also contains records concerning hospitalisation and mental-health intervention.

These matters require particular care.

A hospitalisation, welfare check or police intervention is not automatically evidence of political persecution.

Conversely, the existence of a mental-health intervention does not automatically prove that every decision made during it was lawful or appropriate.

The relevant question is what statutory authority existed at the time, what evidence was relied upon, what procedural safeguards were applied, what information was available, and whether the intervention was proportionate and lawful.

Where the archive alleges that psychiatric processes were deliberately weaponised, that allegation requires evidence of deliberate misuse rather than merely evidence that psychiatric intervention occurred.
	15.	INTERNATIONAL HUMAN RIGHTS LAW

The archive invokes the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, the Convention Against Torture and other international instruments.

These instruments provide an important framework for analysing allegations concerning:

• life and security;

• arbitrary detention;

• torture and cruel, inhuman or degrading treatment;

• privacy;

• freedom of expression;

• equality and non-discrimination;

• effective remedy; and

• protection from arbitrary state interference.

However, international-law terminology should be used precisely.

A claim that conduct violates Article 7 of the ICCPR is not itself a determination by the UN Human Rights Committee.

A claim that conduct amounts to torture is not itself a finding under the Convention Against Torture.

A claim that conduct constitutes a crime against humanity is not itself an ICC determination.

The archive therefore uses international law as an analytical framework and potential avenue of accountability, while preserving the distinction between allegation and international adjudication.
	16.	THE UNITED NATIONS HUMAN RIGHTS COMMITTEE

Australia is subject to the ICCPR framework and has accepted the individual-communications procedure under the First Optional Protocol.

That does not mean that an individual complaint automatically results in an investigation or finding.

The Human Rights Committee’s procedure has admissibility requirements, including that the communication concern a State party that has accepted the Committee’s competence and ordinarily that available domestic remedies have been exhausted.

Accordingly, a possible international strategy is not simply:

“Report Australia and the UN must intervene.”

It is:

• identify the specific ICCPR rights allegedly engaged;

• identify the specific acts or omissions;

• identify responsible authorities;

• document domestic proceedings;

• establish the chronology;

• establish available remedies;

• explain which remedies were pursued;

• explain any alleged barriers to remedies;

• preserve the primary documents;

• address admissibility requirements; and

• present the matter through the appropriate UN procedure.

That is the standard this archive should adopt.
	17.	THE INTERNATIONAL CRIMINAL COURT

The original statement described the ICC as a court to which Barran could simply “file a criminal case”.

That is not the correct description.

Individuals and organisations may provide information concerning alleged crimes within ICC jurisdiction to the Office of the Prosecutor.

The Prosecutor assesses such information against the Court’s jurisdictional and legal requirements.

The ICC has jurisdiction over genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes and aggression, subject to the Rome Statute’s jurisdictional requirements.

Therefore, an ICC communication would have to establish far more than the existence of serious personal harm.

It would need to address:

• jurisdiction;

• temporal jurisdiction;

• territorial or nationality jurisdiction where relevant;

• the elements of the alleged crime;

• the contextual requirements for crimes against humanity where applicable;

• evidence connecting the conduct to those elements;

• complementarity;

• admissibility; and

• the availability and adequacy of domestic processes.

The appropriate description is therefore:

A communication may be made to the Office of the Prosecutor requesting assessment of alleged conduct within ICC jurisdiction.

It is not a guarantee that the ICC will investigate.
	18.	THE HIGH COURT OF AUSTRALIA

The archive also discusses possible proceedings in the High Court of Australia.

The High Court has constitutionally defined jurisdiction.

Section 75 of the Constitution gives the High Court original jurisdiction in specified matters, including matters in which a writ of mandamus or prohibition or an injunction is sought against an officer of the Commonwealth.

That does not mean that every complaint about government conduct can simply be taken directly to the High Court.

Jurisdiction, standing, applicable legislation, procedural rules, limitation issues, existing decisions and the precise relief sought all matter.

Accordingly, the appropriate course is to obtain qualified legal advice about the particular decision or conduct challenged and identify the correct jurisdiction and remedy.

The archive should not promise that a High Court proceeding will produce a particular outcome.

It should document why such a proceeding may be considered and what legal questions require determination.
	19.	INTERNATIONAL PROTECTION AND ASYLUM

The archive discusses the possibility of seeking protection outside Australia.

The existence of serious allegations against Australian institutions does not automatically establish entitlement to refugee status or complementary protection.

Any international protection claim would require assessment under the law of the receiving country and applicable international obligations.

Relevant evidence could include:

• decisions by Australian authorities;

• court and tribunal records;

• evidence of threats or violence;

• evidence concerning state protection;

The archive should therefore present international protection as a possible legal avenue requiring professional assessment rather than as an automatic entitlement.

	22.	WHAT WOULD CONSTITUTE A PROPER INVESTIGATION?

A proper investigation of the archive would not begin by accepting the entire narrative.

Nor would it begin by dismissing it.

It would begin by securing the evidence.

That means:

• preserving original documents;

• recording file names and provenance;

• preserving metadata where available;

• recording dates;

• identifying authors;

• identifying recipients;

• identifying institutional decision-makers;

• constructing a chronological database;

• comparing contradictory records;

	23.	WHAT THE ARCHIVE IS NOT CLAIMING

The archive does not claim that publication itself proves an allegation.

It does not claim that every government official is corrupt.

It does not claim that every police officer, doctor, lawyer, journalist, politician, public servant or service provider participated in wrongdoing.

It does not claim that every adverse decision was unlawful.

It does not claim that every unexplained event was coordinated.

It does not claim that every allegation has been independently corroborated.

It does not claim that a particular person is guilty of a crime merely because that person is named in the archive.

It does not claim that spiritual interpretation constitutes legal evidence.

It does not claim that artificial-intelligence analysis constitutes a judicial finding.

It does not claim that international bodies are obliged to accept the allegations.

The archive makes a narrower claim:

THE RECORD DESERVES TO BE EXAMINED.
	24.	WHAT THE ARCHIVE DOES CLAIM

The archive claims that its documents, testimony and chronology constitute a record of what Barran Dodger says happened to him and of the institutional responses he received.

Where primary documents exist, they can be inspected.

Where official correspondence exists, it can be compared.

Where allegations remain unresolved, they can be identified as unresolved.

Where evidence contradicts the author’s interpretation, that contradiction should be disclosed.

Where the author has drawn an inference rather than possessing direct evidence, the inference should be labelled as such.

Where an institution has provided an explanation, that explanation should be included.

Where an institution has not responded, that should also be recorded accurately.

This is how an archive becomes an evidentiary resource rather than merely an accusation.
	25.	THE QUESTION OF ACCOUNTABILITY

The central accountability question is not:

“Why should institutions believe Barran Dodger?”

Nor is it:

“Why should institutions disbelieve him?”

It is:

“WHAT DOES THE RECORD SHOW?”

That question should be capable of being asked without prejudice.

If the evidence ultimately disproves an allegation, the archive should record that.

If the evidence corroborates an allegation, the archive should record that.

If the evidence is contradictory, the contradiction should remain visible.

If evidence is missing, the gap should be identified.

If an institution acted lawfully, that should be acknowledged.

If an institution acted unlawfully, improperly or unfairly, the evidence should demonstrate it.

If the available evidence cannot establish an allegation, the archive should say so.

That is the standard by which this archive asks to be judged.
	26.	THE HUMAN DIMENSION

Behind every document is a person.

The archive records a life extending across decades.

It records professional work, journalism, art, academic study, mental-health advocacy, writing, relationships, housing, employment, disability support, financial hardship, hospitalisation, legal disputes and repeated attempts to obtain recognition and assistance.

The author describes this history as a process of erasure.

That word is an interpretation.

The underlying evidence is the series of events, decisions, losses, institutional responses and consequences from which that interpretation has been formed.

The distinction matters.

A person can experience profound loss without every allegation surrounding that loss being established.

Equally, an institution can cause serious harm through a lawful-looking administrative process without intending the resulting harm.

The archive therefore asks readers to hold both possibilities open while examining the evidence.

	29.	THE ALLEGATION OF SYSTEMIC ERASURE

The archive uses the concept of “erasure” to describe what the author believes has happened across multiple institutional encounters.

That term encompasses allegations that his identity, employment history, complaints, financial interests, credibility, professional standing, relationships and ability to obtain effective remedies were progressively diminished.

Whether those events constitute a coordinated system is a separate question.

A coordinated conspiracy requires evidence connecting separate acts to a common purpose.

The archive therefore identifies both:

THE PATTERN:

Repeated adverse events and institutional responses.

AND THE QUESTION:

Whether the pattern resulted from coordination, independent administrative decisions, systemic dysfunction, discrimination, error, retaliation, deliberate misconduct, or some combination of these explanations.

That question remains suitable for investigation.
	30.	WHY THE RECORD SHOULD REMAIN OPEN

A historical archive should not close an unresolved question merely because an institution has previously declined to investigate it.

Nor should it treat every institutional refusal as evidence of guilt.

Both conclusions can be premature.

The appropriate response is preservation.

Preserve the document.

Preserve the chronology.

Preserve the response.

Preserve the contradiction.

Preserve the allegation.

Preserve the denial.

Preserve the finding.

Preserve the absence of a finding.

Then allow future investigators to examine the complete record.
	31.	WHAT ACCOUNTABILITY WOULD LOOK LIKE

Accountability does not necessarily mean prosecution.

It can mean:

• an explanation;

• correction of an inaccurate record;

• release of documents;

• independent investigation;

• administrative review;

• restoration of a right;

• compensation where legally available;

• disciplinary action where misconduct is established;

• referral to another jurisdiction;

• recognition of procedural failure;

• protection from further harm;

• preservation of evidence;

• reform of an institutional process; or

• a reasoned determination that an allegation is not substantiated.

Justice requires that the appropriate remedy correspond to the evidence and the legal issue.

The archive therefore seeks examination first.

Determination follows evidence.

Remedy follows determination.
	32.	A REQUEST TO NATIONAL INSTITUTIONS

The archive calls upon relevant Australian institutions to consider the material according to their lawful jurisdiction.

This includes, where appropriate:

• the National Anti-Corruption Commission;

• the Commonwealth Ombudsman;

• the Australian Human Rights Commission;

• relevant Commonwealth departments;

• relevant state oversight bodies;

• police and independent police oversight bodies;

• disability and NDIS oversight mechanisms;

• workers’ compensation and employment authorities;

• courts and tribunals with jurisdiction;

• parliamentary oversight mechanisms; and

• any other body legally empowered to examine particular allegations.

The request is not that every institution accept the author’s conclusions.

The request is that each institution:

RECEIVE.

ASSESS.

RECORD.

EXPLAIN.

REFER WHERE APPROPRIATE.

INVESTIGATE WHERE JURISDICTION AND EVIDENCE REQUIRE IT.

AND PROVIDE REASONS.
	33.	A REQUEST TO INTERNATIONAL BODIES

Where domestic remedies are exhausted or otherwise meet the relevant requirements, international mechanisms may be considered.

Those mechanisms have their own jurisdictional rules and should not be represented as automatic avenues of intervention.

The archive therefore asks international organisations and human-rights bodies to examine the evidence according to their own mandates.

The relevant questions include:

• whether particular rights have been engaged;

• whether effective domestic remedies existed;

• whether those remedies were pursued;

• whether there were barriers to their use;

• whether the alleged conduct falls within the relevant international mechanism’s jurisdiction;

• whether independent corroboration exists; and

• what remedy, if any, is available.
	34.	THE STANDARD OF PROOF

The archive recognises that different institutions use different standards.

A criminal conviction requires proof beyond reasonable doubt.

Civil proceedings generally apply the balance of probabilities, subject to the particular legal context.

Administrative decision-making operates under its governing legislation and applicable standards.

Human-rights bodies apply their own procedures.

Journalists apply evidentiary and editorial standards.

Historians and researchers apply different standards again.

This means that the phrase “proven” must always be accompanied by the question:

PROVEN BY WHOM, UNDER WHAT STANDARD, AND IN WHAT PROCEEDING?

That principle should apply equally to the author and to institutions responding to him.
	35.	THE FINAL QUESTION

After decades of documents, correspondence, complaints, disputes, decisions, allegations, responses and unresolved questions, the central question is now remarkably simple:

WHAT DOES THE COMPLETE RECORD SHOW?

Not what one institution says.

Not what one complainant says.

Not what one journalist says.

Not what one politician says.

Not what an artificial intelligence system says.

Not what a diagnosis says.

Not what a reputation says.

THE RECORD.

The original documents.

The chronology.

The correspondence.

The decisions.

The witnesses.

The contradictions.

The missing information.

The institutional explanations.

The consequences.

And the evidence.

FINAL DECLARATION

I do not ask the reader to believe everything in this archive.

I ask the reader not to dismiss everything in it.

I ask institutions not to confuse rejection of an allegation with investigation of an allegation.

I ask investigators not to confuse a person’s vulnerability with proof that their testimony is false.

I ask supporters not to confuse testimony with established fact.

I ask critics not to confuse disagreement with disproof.

I ask journalists to examine primary documents.

I ask lawyers to identify the precise legal questions.

I ask public authorities to identify their jurisdiction and reasons.

I ask human-rights organisations to assess the evidence against the standards they are required to apply.

And I ask future investigators to preserve the distinction between what is known, what is alleged, what is inferred and what remains unknown.

The purpose of this archive is not to manufacture a verdict.

It is to prevent the record from disappearing.

If the allegations are wrong, the evidence should demonstrate why.

If the allegations are partly correct, the record should identify what occurred.

If serious wrongdoing occurred, the evidence should not be buried because it is institutionally inconvenient.

If the evidence remains inconclusive, that conclusion should also be recorded.

That is accountability.

That is procedural fairness.

That is the minimum standard owed to any person who places a serious allegation before the institutions of a democratic society.

The archive therefore remains open.

The evidence remains preserved.

The allegations remain identified as allegations where they have not been independently determined.

The official responses remain part of the record.

And the unanswered questions remain questions until the evidence resolves them.
