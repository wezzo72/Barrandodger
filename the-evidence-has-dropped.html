Why the Federal Court did not allocate the disclosure

On 7 March 2023 — the Court’s own officer fixes that date — I wrote to Sia Lagos, Chief Executive Officer and Principal Registrar, a person able to receive a public-interest disclosure. The letter is the 3 March 2023 letter to Sia Lagos. I was asking for help and protection because I feared further suffering and imminent danger to my life.

Scott Tredwell answered on 27 March 2023, on the letterhead of the Federal Court of Australia, Principal Registry, Harry Gibbs Commonwealth Law Courts, Brisbane. The letter is 2023-03-27-federal-court-final-assessment-dr-rich-mclean.pdf. He says he is an officer authorised under the Public Interest Disclosure Act 2013 (Cth). The spelling on the letter is Tredwell.

The reason the disclosure was not allocated is written in his decision paragraph, not in a missing page. He gives two grounds, and he does not reconcile them with the employment sentence above them. First, he is not satisfied the disclosure was made by a public official. Second, he is not satisfied it was provided to an authorised recipient, because he is satisfied the conduct disclosed does not relate in any way to the Federal Court, the Federal Circuit and Family Court, or the National Native Title Tribunal. On those two grounds he says there is no reasonable basis to decide the disclosure is an internal disclosure under subsection 43(2), and that he must not allocate the handling of it to any agency. He also writes that he will not advise the Principal Officers of the disclosure or of his decision. The only door he leaves open is a complaint to the Commonwealth Ombudsman — the office already on this file, and the office that later restricted contact.

As I am not satisfied that the disclosure made in your correspondence and other information which was received by the Federal Court and FCFCOA was made by a public official or provided to an authorised recipient under the PID Act, there is no reasonable basis on which I can decide that the disclosure is an internal disclosure; subsection 43(2) of the PID Act.

As I am not satisfied that your disclosure meets the requirement of the PID Act to be an internal disclosure, I must decide (in the language used in section 43 of the PID Act as above) not to allocate the handling of that disclosure to any agency.

This means that no further action under the PID Act will be taken by the Federal Court or FCFCOA, or any other Commonwealth agency on the correspondence and other information you provided to the Federal Court or FCFCOA.
I am satisfied from the information that you provided that the conduct disclosed in your email correspondence and attached documents does not relate in any way to the Federal Court, FCFCOA or the NNTT. As a result, I am not satisfied that the disclosure made in your correspondence and other information which was received by the Federal Court and FCFCOA was provided to an authorised recipient.

That is the reason. Not a finding that the conduct did not happen. A classification: not a public official, or not the right door, and therefore no agency will be told. I had asked for protection. The letter that records the danger is the letter that withholds it.

The three things he was prepared to assume

Before that refusal he records what the information tended to show, in his words. He does not say he has proved it. He says he is satisfied that I believe it, that it is difficult to say whether the belief is on reasonable grounds, and that for the initial assessment he is prepared to assume the conduct is disclosable.

I am satisfied, from the correspondence and other information provided, that you believe that the information you have disclosed tends to show one or more instances of conduct that:

perverts, or is engaged in for the purpose of perverting, or attempting to pervert, the course of justice; section 29 Item 3(a) PID Act;

conduct that constitutes maladministration; section 29 Item 4 PID Act; and

conduct that unreasonably results in a danger to the health or safety of one or more persons; or unreasonably results in, or increases, a risk of danger to the health or safety of one or more persons; section 29 Item 8 PID Act.

It is difficult to determine whether this belief is based on reasonable grounds or not. However, for the purposes of the initial assessment I am prepared to assume that the conduct disclosed in your correspondence and other information received is disclosable conduct for the purposes of the PID Act.

Perverting the course of justice. Maladministration. A danger, or an increased risk of danger, to health or safety. He was prepared to treat that as disclosable long enough to decide what to do with me. What he did was refuse to allocate it. Two pages earlier he is satisfied I am, or was, a DSS employee. In the decision paragraph he is not satisfied the disclosure was made by a public official. The letter never explains how both can stand.

On the information you have provided me, I am satisfied that you are, or were, an employee with the Department of Social Services, providing services under the trading name Rich McLean, Arts Life Coach, Peer-Support Worker & Mental Health Advocate.

That sentence sits against DSS, 1 July 2023, against Comcare, 26 May 2021, against Sarah LeMaire, 3 June 2021, and against McLean and Comcare, 12 July 2023. I say the employment sentence should have overturned the lesser finding. It did not. A letter can be lawful in form and still be a moral failure. I call the refusal callous, and I call it cowardly: bureaucratic language dressed as legitimacy, used to take back in the decision paragraph what the employment paragraph had just given.
