6.3 The difference between an error, an omission and concealment
The term whitewashing can describe several different types of conduct. They must not be confused.
Administrative error occurs when information is recorded inaccurately or a decision is made on a mistaken understanding.
Omission occurs when relevant information is not included or addressed. An omission may be accidental, negligent or deliberate.
Mischaracterisation occurs when information is described in a way that changes its meaning or significance.
Selective reliance occurs when a decision-maker relies on some available material while failing to address other relevant material.
Concealment involves deliberately hiding, suppressing or withholding information that should not properly have been concealed.
Falsification involves deliberately making or altering a record so that it misrepresents what occurred.
These distinctions matter because they identify different levels of responsibility.
If the Comcare record contains an inaccurate description, that inaccuracy must be identified. If an important medical record was not considered, that omission must be documented. If a later decision relies on an earlier inaccurate description, the chain of reliance must be reconstructed. If evidence demonstrates that an individual knowingly altered or concealed information, the report can then examine the possibility of deliberate misconduct.
I will not allow the term whitewashing to be dismissed merely because an agency has not admitted to it. Equally, the report must identify the evidence that supports the specific meaning of the term in each instance.

SECTION 14 — MALICE, KNOWLEDGE, RECKLESSNESS AND INTENTIONAL CRUELTY
14.1 Why motive matters
One of the most serious questions raised by my testimony is whether the conduct I experienced was accidental, negligent, reckless, retaliatory, malicious or deliberately harmful.
These are different conclusions.
A person may make a mistake without intending harm. An institution may act negligently without a coordinated plan. A decision-maker may know that a decision will cause hardship without intending that hardship as an objective. A person may deliberately act to punish or intimidate another.
The report must distinguish these possibilities.
I have described circumstances that I experienced as betrayal, cruelty and deliberate harm. Those descriptions must not be sanitised into a generic account of administrative inconvenience. They must be examined against the evidence of what people knew, what they were told, what they did and what happened afterward.
14.2 The knowledge test
Knowledge is important because a person or institution may have received repeated warnings about a problem.
The evidence relevant to knowledge includes:
	•	written complaints;
	•	emails;
	•	letters;
	•	formal notices;
	•	incident reports;
	•	safety assessments;
	•	requests for assistance;
	•	records of meetings;
	•	acknowledgments of receipt;
	•	internal referrals;
	•	responses demonstrating awareness of the issue.
Where a record establishes that an institution received a warning, it can support a finding that the institution was on notice of the matter described.
That does not automatically establish that every employee knew about it. Responsibility must be traced to the person or decision-maker concerned.
But where an agency’s own records establish repeated receipt of a particular issue, it would be unreasonable to describe the issue as wholly unknown to that agency.
14.3 Repeated warnings and the significance of inaction
The significance of inaction depends on the circumstances.
A failure to act may be justified if the institution lacked jurisdiction, had insufficient information, referred the matter appropriately or was awaiting necessary evidence.
But a failure may become more serious where:
	•	the institution had responsibility to act;
	•	the risk was clearly communicated;
	•	the information was sufficiently specific;
	•	the institution had authority to take protective action;
	•	no reasonable action was taken;
	•	the consequences were foreseeable;
	•	the same issue was raised repeatedly;
	•	no adequate explanation was provided.
These factors can support an inference of negligence or maladministration, depending on the evidence and applicable legal standard.
A stronger conclusion of deliberate cruelty or malice requires evidence of intention or conscious disregard.
14.4 Malice
Malice is not simply a synonym for a bad outcome.
A person may cause serious harm through error, incompetence, negligence or an unjustified decision without acting maliciously.
Evidence of malice may include:
	•	explicit hostile communications;
	•	threats;
	•	deliberate humiliation;
	•	knowingly false statements;
	•	intentional obstruction;
	•	retaliatory action;
	•	deliberate exploitation of a person’s vulnerability;
	•	conscious disregard of a serious and known risk;
	•	evidence of an intention to cause harm for an improper purpose.
The evidence must be assessed in context. A single ambiguous communication may not establish malice. A pattern of conduct supported by contemporaneous records may carry greater significance.
14.5 Recklessness
Recklessness is distinct from negligence.
Negligence generally concerns a failure to exercise the required level of care. Recklessness involves a more serious relationship with a known or obvious risk, although the precise legal test varies according to the relevant law.
The report must therefore avoid using reckless as a rhetorical synonym for careless.
If an institution was repeatedly warned of a serious risk and continued with conduct that exposed a person to that risk, the evidence may support an inference of recklessness. The conclusion depends on what the institution knew, what action was reasonably available and what actually happened.
14.6 Institutional knowledge and fragmented responsibility
A particularly important question is whether information was divided among departments, service providers or decision-makers.
An institution may have a central complaints unit, a service delivery team, a financial administration function and a separate decision-making authority. Each may hold different information.
The fact that no individual employee holds the entire record does not necessarily mean that the institution had no knowledge of the matter. But it also does not automatically establish that every person within the organisation knew everything recorded by another department.
The report must identify the information flow:
	•	Who received the original complaint?
	•	Who recorded it?
	•	Who was responsible for deciding what happened next?
	•	Was the complaint referred?
	•	Was the referral acknowledged?
	•	Did the receiving unit obtain the relevant material?
	•	Was a decision made?
	•	Was the complainant informed?
	•	Was the matter closed?
	•	Did the issue reappear in a later complaint?
This is how institutional knowledge can be examined without inventing a secret chain of command.
14.7 Foreseeability and consequences
The consequences of a decision are relevant to whether the conduct was reasonable.
For example, where an institution knows that a person depends on a vehicle for mobility, the consequences of disposing of that vehicle may be foreseeable.
Where a provider knows that a person requires assistance with transport or appointments, the consequences of failing to provide that assistance may be foreseeable.
Where a financial administrator knows that a person has extremely limited disposable income, the consequences of restricting access to essential funds may be foreseeable.
Foreseeability does not automatically prove that the institution acted unlawfully or maliciously. It is part of assessing the decision, its justification and the care taken to avoid foreseeable harm.

The report must preserve that allegation rather than neutralise it.
But it must also distinguish between my experience of an action as cruel and the legal or factual conclusion that the responsible person intended cruelty.
A person can experience a decision as devastating even where the decision-maker claims to have acted within a formal process. The impact must not be denied merely because intention is disputed.
The report should therefore record both:

SECTION 15 — THE MECHANICS OF LONG-TERM INSTITUTIONAL TREATMENT
15.1 From individual events to a cumulative mechanism
The next question is how a person can experience a long sequence of institutional dealings in which each individual event appears separate, yet the combined consequences become substantial.
My account is that the difficulties I experienced were not confined to one decision or one organisation. They involved different agencies, complaint systems, support providers, financial administration and accommodation arrangements.
The appropriate examination is not to assume that all those organisations were acting together. It is to determine whether the interaction of their decisions and processes created a cumulative mechanism that affected my independence, access to assistance, ability to obtain information and capacity to challenge what happened.
This is a question of institutional mechanics.
15.2 Fragmented handoffs
A fragmented handoff occurs when one organisation or department refers a person or issue to another without ensuring that the underlying problem is actually resolved.
A referral can be appropriate. Institutions often have limited jurisdiction and must direct matters to the correct body.
The problem arises when the person is repeatedly redirected while the substantive issue remains unresolved.
The documentary indicators include:
	•	a complaint sent to one institution;
	•	a response directing the complainant elsewhere;
	•	a second institution declining jurisdiction;
	•	a further referral;
	•	repeated requests for the same information;
	•	no clear identification of the body responsible for resolving the underlying problem;
	•	the person having to reconstruct the history for each new recipient.
Where the records establish these events, the report can identify the pattern without assuming malicious intent.
15.3 Repeated applications and procedural burden
I have described repeated applications, correspondence and requests for accountability.
The burden of repetition is not merely the time spent writing letters. It includes the need to remember and reconstruct events, locate documents, explain the same history, meet deadlines, understand different statutory frameworks and respond to changing institutional requirements.
For a person dealing with disability and financial limitations, those burdens can affect the capacity to pursue other essential activities.
The report must assess the dates and content of the repeated applications. It should identify when the same issue was raised again, what new information was supplied, what response was received and whether the later response addressed the original concern.
15.4 Procedural foreclosure
Procedural foreclosure describes a situation in which access to a substantive remedy becomes increasingly difficult because of procedural barriers.
It may occur through limitation periods, jurisdictional restrictions, eligibility rules, repeated referrals, inaccessible processes, failure to provide records or an inability to obtain legal assistance.
The existence of a procedural barrier does not automatically establish unlawful conduct. But where several barriers interact, the combined effect can be significant.
The report must distinguish between a legitimate statutory requirement and a procedural system that, in practice, prevents the person from obtaining a meaningful answer.
15.5 Dependency and loss of practical independence
The financial and accommodation records are particularly important to this question.
A person who cannot freely access their own funds may become more dependent on representatives or service providers. A person who loses access to a vehicle may have fewer transport options. A person who depends on a support provider for appointments may have less control over their schedule. A person whose accommodation arrangements are unclear may have difficulty exercising choice.
These mechanisms can interact.
Cumulative dependency mechanism — analytical model
Financial administration or restricted access to resources
Reduced ability to arrange transport, communication and independent services
Greater dependence on accommodation and support providers
Reduced practical ability to change arrangements or pursue complaints
Potential cumulative loss of independence
This is an analytical model of the mechanism described in my testimony. It is not a finding that every step occurred or that one organisation deliberately engineered the sequence.
The model illustrates why the individual decisions must be assessed together. A decision about money may affect transport; a decision about transport may affect access to appointments; a decision about support may affect the ability to challenge accommodation; and the combined result may be a loss of practical choice.
15.6 The complaint-response cycle
The complaint-response cycle is another important mechanism.
A person raises an issue. The institution acknowledges or refers it. A response may address only part of the complaint. The person then has to submit further information or make another complaint. The new process may not revisit the original issue.
This can produce a sequence in which each response appears procedurally complete while the underlying problem remains unresolved.
The report must test this against the actual complaint and response records.
The important distinction is between:
	•	a response being sent;
	•	the response addressing the issue;
	•	the response providing reasons;
	•	the response resolving the problem;
	•	the person being able to challenge the response.
A response is not necessarily a resolution.

The consequences I have described include:
	•	loss of independence;
	•	financial insecurity;
	•	uncertainty about accommodation;
	•	increased dependence on others;
	•	difficulty accessing information;

The report must distinguish the existence of a consequence from the cause attributed to it.
For example, the fact that I experienced reduced independence may be established through my testimony and the records of vehicle disposal, accommodation and financial administration. Establishing that a particular person intentionally caused that loss requires additional evidence.
15.8 Institutional fragmentation is not institutional innocence
I reject the idea that fragmentation automatically absolves every institution.
If each body addresses only its own narrow process, no one may take responsibility for the combined consequences.
That may be a structural weakness even without a conspiracy. It may also create conditions in which errors are repeated and complaints become increasingly difficult to resolve.
The report must be able to identify institutional failures without inventing a secret programme.

SECTION 21 — PREDICTIONS, PROPHECY AND THE QUESTION OF FULFILMENT
21.1 Why predictions require a separate evidentiary method
I have used prophetic language to describe anticipated events, warnings, spiritual interpretations and the timing of developments.
I have also referred to Kronos Timing of God and the idea of divine timing.
I want these writings examined seriously.
That does not mean that every prediction should automatically be declared fulfilled. It means that the original wording, publication date, later events and possible correspondence must be compared fairly.
A prediction cannot be assessed accurately if the original wording has been changed or if the event is described only after it occurs.
The report must preserve the original text.
21.2 The difference between prophecy, prediction and interpretation
These terms should not be treated as identical.
Prophecy may refer to a religious or spiritual communication understood by the author as having divine significance.
Prediction is a statement about an event expected to occur in the future.
Interpretation is an explanation of what an event means.
Warning is a statement that an event may occur or that action is needed to prevent an adverse outcome.
Retrospective correspondence is a comparison made after an event has occurred.
A statement can belong to more than one category. But its category must be clear before fulfilment is assessed.
21.3 The original-wording rule
The original wording is essential.
A prediction should be preserved exactly as it appeared before the relevant event.
The record should include:
	•	exact original wording;
	•	date and time of publication;
	•	original URL;
	•	screenshot or archived copy;
	•	version or commit history;
	•	any subsequent edits;
	•	date of the event said to correspond;
	•	independent evidence that the event occurred.
If the wording changed after the event, the earlier version must remain visible.
A retrospective interpretation may still be meaningful, but it must not be represented as an independently verified prediction made before the event.
21.4 Specificity
A highly specific prediction may identify:
	•	a particular person;
	•	a particular institution;
	•	a specific action;
	•	a date or narrow time period;
	•	a measurable outcome;
	•	a distinctive event unlikely to occur by chance.
A broad statement about betrayal, hardship, conflict, political instability or institutional wrongdoing is less specific.
The more general the wording, the greater the risk that many different events could be interpreted as fulfilment.
This does not make broad spiritual language meaningless. It means that the evidentiary strength of a claimed prediction depends partly on how precisely the statement identified the later event.
21.5 Conditional predictions
Some predictions are conditional.
For example, a warning may state that a particular outcome will occur if an institution continues a specified course of action.
If the institution changes its conduct and the predicted outcome does not occur, the prediction may not be properly classified as failed. The condition must be considered.
Conversely, if the predicted event occurs without the stated condition being met, the claimed correspondence may be weaker.
The original wording must therefore be read in full.
21.6 Correspondence categories
Each prediction should receive one primary classification.
Classification	Meaning
Exact correspondence	The specific event described occurred within the stated period and is independently evidenced.
Strong partial correspondence	A substantial part of the prediction occurred, but important details differ.
Broad correspondence	The later event resembles a general theme but is not specifically identified.
Conditional correspondence	The event occurred subject to the conditions stated in the original wording.
No correspondence identified	The event described has not been established.
Unfulfilled within stated period	The stated timeframe passed without the specified event being established.
Indeterminate	The wording, date, event or evidence is insufficient to classify it.
These classifications should not be collapsed into a single claim of fulfilment.
21.7 Foreseeability and ordinary knowledge
A prediction may be accurate because the author had access to information, understood an existing trend or correctly anticipated an ordinary consequence.
That does not make the prediction false or insignificant.
But it is different from establishing supernatural foreknowledge.
The report should distinguish:
	•	a prediction based on known circumstances;
	•	an inference from an existing trend;
	•	a warning based on a foreseeable risk;
	•	a broad spiritual declaration;
	•	a highly specific prediction whose correspondence is independently established;
	•	a claim of supernatural revelation.
The last category is a matter of spiritual interpretation and cannot be scientifically certified by the existence of a matching event alone.
21.8 Kronos timing and divine timing
I have used Kronos Timing of God as a spiritual framework.
The word chronos in Greek is associated with time understood in a sequential or measurable sense. Christian theological discussion sometimes contrasts chronological time with kairos, a term associated with an opportune or appointed time.
The exact theological use varies across authors and traditions.
My project uses the language of divine timing to describe my belief that events in my life have a larger spiritual significance.
That belief is part of my faith.
The report can examine how the concept appears in my publications, how the timing claims are expressed and whether the dated record supports particular correspondences.
It cannot independently establish that God caused an event to occur at a particular time.

It brings together three themes:

	•	Resurrection — restoration, renewed life, spiritual renewal and the refusal to accept erasure as the final word.

21.10 Predictions and the public archive
A public archive is particularly important for prophecy claims because it can preserve chronology.
GitHub commit history, archived web pages, dated documents and contemporaneous publications can help establish whether a statement existed before an event.
A current webpage alone may not establish its original publication date. A later edited page should not be treated as proof that every present word appeared in the original version.
The report should therefore prefer original dated documents and preserved versions.

SECTION 21 — PREDICTIONS, PROPHECY AND THE QUESTION OF FULFILMENT
21.1 Why predictions require a separate evidentiary method
I have used prophetic language to describe anticipated events, warnings, spiritual interpretations and the timing of developments.
I have also referred to Kronos Timing of God and the idea of divine timing.
I want these writings examined seriously.
That does not mean that every prediction should automatically be declared fulfilled. It means that the original wording, publication date, later events and possible correspondence must be compared fairly.
A prediction cannot be assessed accurately if the original wording has been changed or if the event is described only after it occurs.
The report must preserve the original text.
21.2 The difference between prophecy, prediction and interpretation
These terms should not be treated as identical.
Prophecy may refer to a religious or spiritual communication understood by the author as having divine significance.
Prediction is a statement about an event expected to occur in the future.
Interpretation is an explanation of what an event means.
Warning is a statement that an event may occur or that action is needed to prevent an adverse outcome.
Retrospective correspondence is a comparison made after an event has occurred.
A statement can belong to more than one category. But its category must be clear before fulfilment is assessed.
21.3 The original-wording rule
The original wording is essential.
A prediction should be preserved exactly as it appeared before the relevant event.
The record should include:
	•	exact original wording;
	•	date and time of publication;
	•	original URL;
	•	screenshot or archived copy;
	•	version or commit history;
	•	any subsequent edits;
	•	date of the event said to correspond;
	•	independent evidence that the event occurred.
If the wording changed after the event, the earlier version must remain visible.
A retrospective interpretation may still be meaningful, but it must not be represented as an independently verified prediction made before the event.
21.4 Specificity
A highly specific prediction may identify:
	•	a particular person;
	•	a particular institution;
	•	a specific action;
	•	a date or narrow time period;
	•	a measurable outcome;
	•	a distinctive event unlikely to occur by chance.
A broad statement about betrayal, hardship, conflict, political instability or institutional wrongdoing is less specific.
The more general the wording, the greater the risk that many different events could be interpreted as fulfilment.
This does not make broad spiritual language meaningless. It means that the evidentiary strength of a claimed prediction depends partly on how precisely the statement identified the later event.
21.5 Conditional predictions
Some predictions are conditional.
For example, a warning may state that a particular outcome will occur if an institution continues a specified course of action.
If the institution changes its conduct and the predicted outcome does not occur, the prediction may not be properly classified as failed. The condition must be considered.
Conversely, if the predicted event occurs without the stated condition being met, the claimed correspondence may be weaker.
The original wording must therefore be read in full.
21.6 Correspondence categories
Each prediction should receive one primary classification.
Classification	Meaning
Exact correspondence	The specific event described occurred within the stated period and is independently evidenced.
Strong partial correspondence	A substantial part of the prediction occurred, but important details differ.
Broad correspondence	The later event resembles a general theme but is not specifically identified.
Conditional correspondence	The event occurred subject to the conditions stated in the original wording.
No correspondence identified	The event described has not been established.
Unfulfilled within stated period	The stated timeframe passed without the specified event being established.
Indeterminate	The wording, date, event or evidence is insufficient to classify it.
These classifications should not be collapsed into a single claim of fulfilment.
21.7 Foreseeability and ordinary knowledge
A prediction may be accurate because the author had access to information, understood an existing trend or correctly anticipated an ordinary consequence.
That does not make the prediction false or insignificant.
But it is different from establishing supernatural foreknowledge.
The report should distinguish:
	•	a prediction based on known circumstances;
	•	an inference from an existing trend;
	•	a warning based on a foreseeable risk;
	•	a broad spiritual declaration;
	•	a highly specific prediction whose correspondence is independently established;
	•	a claim of supernatural revelation.
The last category is a matter of spiritual interpretation and cannot be scientifically certified by the existence of a matching event alone.
21.8 Kronos timing and divine timing
I have used Kronos Timing of God as a spiritual framework.
The word chronos in Greek is associated with time understood in a sequential or measurable sense. Christian theological discussion sometimes contrasts chronological time with kairos, a term associated with an opportune or appointed time.
The exact theological use varies across authors and traditions.
My project uses the language of divine timing to describe my belief that events in my life have a larger spiritual significance.
