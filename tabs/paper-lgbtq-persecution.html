Sexual Persecution and Political Power: A 
Comprehensive Analysis of LGBTQ+ History 
in Australian Democracy (1972-2025) 
An Academic Examination of Persecution, Public

Abstract 
This paper examines the complex intersection of sexuality, 
political power, and social persecution in Australian history 
from 1972 to 2025. Through systematic analysis of legal 
reforms, hate crimes, political scandals, and the experiences 
of openly LGBTQ+ public figures, this research 
demonstrates how sexuality has been weaponized as a tool 
of social control, political manipulation, and institutional 
violence. The study explores five interconnected 
dimensions: (1) systematic persecution and criminalization 
of LGBTQ+ individuals; (2) pioneering openly LGBTQ+ 
figures in public life; (3) political scandals involving 
sexuality; (4) the reclamation of derogatory language; and 
(5) ongoing human rights challenges. Drawing from 

government records, academic sources, legal documents, 
and firsthand testimony, this paper documents a 50-year 
trajectory from criminalization to partial acceptance, while 
acknowledging persistent institutional discrimination and 
violence against LGBTQ+ Australians. 
Keywords: LGBTQ+ persecution, Australian political 
history, hate crimes, sexual orientation discrimination, 
queer theory, human rights violations, political scandals, 
linguistic reclamation

1. Introduction: The Politics of Sexual 
Persecution in Australian Democracy 
In Australian political and social history, few factors have 
been as systematically weaponized for the purposes of 
persecution, character assassination, and social control as 
sexual orientation and gender identity. From colonial-era 
death penalties for sodomy to contemporary digital 
persecution campaigns, LGBTQ+ Australians have faced 
unique forms of state-sanctioned violence, institutional 
discrimination, and social marginalization (MoAD, 2015). 
This paper examines what might be termed the "sexual 
persecution complex" in Australian democracy—a multi-
layered system through which sexuality has been deployed 
as: 
1.A criminal category (legal persecution through 
sodomy laws, 1788-1997) 
2.A political weapon (scandals, character assassination, 
career destruction) 
3.A medical pathology (psychiatric categorization and 
conversion therapy) 
4.A moral panic (especially during the AIDS crisis) 
5.A site of violence (hate crimes, murders, police 
brutality) 
6.A barrier to citizenship (discrimination in 
employment, housing, family rights) 

The research question driving this analysis is: Who, across 
documented Australian history, has experienced the 
most notorious, systematic, or high-profile persecution 
specifically related to their sexual orientation or gender 
identity? 
The answer, as this paper demonstrates, is not a single 
individual but rather multiple interconnected categories of 
persecution—from martyred figures like Dr. George 
Duncan whose 1972 murder catalyzed legal reform, to 
pioneering politicians like Bob Brown and Michael Kirby 
who faced career obstacles and public vilification, to 
contemporary whistleblowers experiencing coordinated 
state persecution. 
2. Historical Context: Criminalization and the 
Death Penalty (Pre-1975) 
2.1 Colonial Legal Framework 
From the establishment of British colonial rule in 1788, 
Australian law inherited English sodomy statutes that 
classified certain sexual acts between men as capital 
offenses. "Until 1899 in Australia, the death penalty could 
be applied in cases of buggery or sodomy. For many years 
thereafter, the maximum penalty was life in prison" 
(MoAD, 2015). 
Victoria maintained the death penalty for sodomy until 
1949—a full century after most Western democracies had 
abolished execution for sexual offenses. This legal 
framework created a climate of extreme fear and 

persecution, driving LGBTQ+ individuals into absolute 
secrecy or forcing them into exile (MoAD, 2015). 
2.2 Continuing Arrests in the Reform Era 
Even as penalties gradually reduced, enforcement 
continued. "Change came slowly and individuals were still 
being arrested, charged and convicted for homosexual acts 
during the 1970s," despite growing international human 
rights advocacy and domestic reform movements (MoAD, 
2015). 
The federal parliament passed a motion supporting 
decriminalization in 1973, but as legislative power rested 
with states and territories, this motion had no practical 
effect. Australia remained a patchwork of criminalization, 
with LGBTQ+ individuals facing radically different legal 
status depending on their geographic location. 
3. The Catalyst for Change: Dr. George 
Duncan and the 1972 Murder 
3.1 The Crime 
On May 10, 1972, Dr. George Ian Ogilvie Duncan, a 41-
year-old law lecturer at the University of Adelaide, was 
murdered by being thrown into the Torrens River in 
Adelaide, South Australia. Dr. Duncan drowned after being 
violently thrown from the riverbank at a location known as 
a gay cruising area or "beat" (The Guardian, 2022; ABC 
News, 2022). 

A witness described Duncan's final moments: "And then he 
just slipped away ... the river swallowed him" (The 
Guardian, 2022). Dr. Duncan's body was found early the 
next morning. 
3.2 Police Involvement and Cover-up 
The case became notorious when evidence emerged 
suggesting police involvement in the murder. According to 
investigations and witness testimony: 
•The Torrens River location was a well-known meeting 
place for gay men 
•Police had engaged in systematic harassment and 
violence at such locations 
•Three police officers were charged with Duncan's 
murder but were acquitted despite circumstantial 
evidence 
•The case remained officially "unsolved" for decades, 
though widely believed to involve police perpetrators 
(BBC, 2017; ABC News, 2022) 
3.3 Catalyzing Reform 
Dr. Duncan's murder became a turning point in Australian 
LGBTQ+ history: 
"The 1972 murder by drowning in Adelaide's 
Torrens River of a homosexual academic, 
Dr George Duncan, raised public awareness 
of widespread harassment of homosexuals 
and resulted in a push for law reform by 
some parliamentarians and by the gay 
rights organisation known as the Campaign 

Against Moral Persecution (CAMP)" 
(MoAD, 2015). 
The death galvanized public opinion, media attention, and 
political action in ways that decades of quiet advocacy had 
not achieved. Within three years, South Australia became 
the first state to fully decriminalize homosexuality—a 
direct response to the outrage over Duncan's killing. 
3.4 Historical Significance 
Dr. George Duncan represents perhaps the most 
consequential single victim of anti-gay violence in 
Australian history. His murder: 
1.Exposed police violence against LGBTQ+ individuals 
2.Demonstrated the deadly consequences of 
criminalization 
3.Mobilized the nascent gay rights movement 
4.Provided political cover for reformist politicians like 
Don Dunstan 
5.Changed the trajectory of LGBTQ+ legal rights in 
Australia 
A memorial plaque near the site of his death reads: "In 
memory of Dr George Duncan, whose death by drowning in 
the River Torrens on 10th May 1972 started a movement 
that led to the decriminalisation of homosexuality in South 
Australia on 5th September 1975" (Wikipedia, 2024). 
4. Decriminalization Timeline: 22 Years of 
Uneven Progress (1975-1997) 
4.1 South Australia: The Pioneer (1975) 

September 17, 1975: South Australia became the first 
Australian state to decriminalize male homosexuality under 
the leadership of Premier Don Dunstan and Attorney-
General Peter Duncan. 
The Criminal Law (Sexual Offences) Amendment Act 1975 
abolished: 
•Offenses of buggery 
•Gross indecency charges 
•Soliciting provisions 
•Created an equal age of consent for homosexual and 
heterosexual acts (MoAD, 2015) 
Don Dunstan (1926-1999), who later came out as bisexual, 
had been working toward this reform since his tenure as 
Attorney-General in the mid-1960s. He strategically waited 
for public opinion to shift following the Duncan murder 
before introducing comprehensive reform (The Big Smoke, 
2021). 
4.2 The National Decriminalization Sequence 
The decriminalization process took 22 years to spread 
across all Australian states and territories (ABC News, 
2015): 
1.Australian Capital Territory (November 4, 1976) - 
Nearly beat SA but delayed by lack of self-government 
2.Victoria (December 23, 1980) - Under Liberal premier 
Dick Hamer, though a poorly worded "soliciting" 
clause allowed continued police harassment 
3.Northern Territory (October 4, 1983) - Only 
jurisdiction without a grassroots reform campaign 

4.New South Wales (May 22, 1984) - Anti-
discrimination laws passed in 1982, creating a two-year 
period where being gay was protected from 
employment discrimination but still criminal 
5.Western Australia (December 7, 1989 / March 1990) - 
After four failed attempts in the 1970s-80s 
6.Queensland (November 29, 1990) - Reform 
impossible under Premier Joh Bjelke-Petersen; 
changed immediately after Labor's Wayne Goss elected 
7.Tasmania (May 13, 1997) - Last state to decriminalize, 
only after High Court intervention and by a single vote 
in the Upper House 
4.3 Disparate Regional Experiences 
This 22-year gap created profound disparities. An LGBTQ+ 
individual in South Australia enjoyed legal protection from 
1975, while an identical person in Tasmania faced potential 
life imprisonment until 1997. This legal patchwork: 
•Forced internal migration to more progressive states 
•Created "law reform refugees" within their own 
country 
•Demonstrated the inadequacy of federalism for human 
rights protection 
•Illustrated how conservative state governments could 
delay equality for decades despite national consensus 
5. Pioneering LGBTQ+ Political Figures 
5.1 Don Dunstan (1926-1999): The Architect of 
Reform 

Don Dunstan, Premier of South Australia (1967-68, 
1970-79), stands as arguably the most consequential 
political ally of LGBTQ+ rights in Australian history. As 
Attorney-General in the 1960s, Dunstan drafted reform 
legislation but strategically delayed introduction until 
public opinion could support it. 
Following Dr. Duncan's murder, Dunstan moved swiftly to 
pass the most comprehensive decriminalization legislation 
in the British Commonwealth at that time (The Big Smoke, 
2021). Later in life, Dunstan publicly identified as bisexual, 
adding personal dimension to his political advocacy. 
5.2 Bob Brown (b. 1944): First Openly Gay Federal 
Parliamentarian 
Dr. Bob Brown was the first openly gay member of the 
Parliament of Australia and the first openly gay leader of an 
Australian political party. Brown served in the Australian 
Senate from 1996 to 2012 and led the Australian Greens 
from 2005 to 2012 (Wikipedia, 2024). 
Brown's political trajectory: 
•1972: Co-founded the world's first Green Party in 
Tasmania 
•1983: Elected to Tasmanian Parliament while openly 
gay—the first openly gay parliamentarian in Australia 
•1996: Elected to federal Senate 
•2002: Joined in Senate by Penny Wong, Australia's first 
openly lesbian federal parliamentarian (MoAD, 2017) 
Brown faced persistent homophobia throughout his career, 
including: 

•Public attacks from conservative politicians 
•Media scrutiny of his personal life 
•Dismissive treatment of his policy positions as 
"radical" due to his sexuality 
•Physical threats and hate mail 
Brown has stated: "I'm an environmental icon, a political 
icon, maybe a gay icon, but really, I'm just Bob Brown. I'm 
just me" (Peppermint Magazine, 2023). 
5.3 Justice Michael Kirby (b. 1939): Highest Judicial 
Office 
The Honourable Michael Donald Kirby AC CMG served 
as a Justice of the High Court of Australia from 1996 to 
2009—the first openly gay High Court Justice in Australian 
history (Wikipedia, 2024; EBSCO, 2024). 
Personal Life and Coming Out: 
•Openly gay since approximately 1984 
•Partnered with Johan van Vloten since 1969 (50+ 
years) 
•Publicly listed van Vloten as his partner in Who's Who 
in Australia in 1999 
•Married van Vloten on February 11, 2019 
Judicial Career: 
•Kirby's dissent rate was the highest in High Court 
history—reflecting his progressive legal interpretations 
•Known for incorporating international human rights 
law into Australian jurisprudence 
•Consistently advocated for marginalized groups in his 
judgments 

Advocacy and International Work: 
•Keynote speaker at Sydney Gay Games VI (2002) 
•President of the International Commission of Jurists 
•Led UN inquiry into North Korean human rights 
abuses (2013-2014) 
•Recipient of Japan's Order of the Rising Sun (2017) 
Persecution and Obstacles: 
Justice Kirby's brother, David Kirby (also a judge), revealed 
in 2017 that Michael had received societal messages from 
his early teens that his sexuality should be "a source of deep 
shame," leading him to abandon any thought of a political 
career (Sydney Morning Herald, 2017). This demonstrates 
how even the most accomplished openly gay Australians 
faced systematic barriers. 
In November 2007, Kirby publicly accused Anglican 
Archbishop Peter Jensen and Catholic Archbishop George 
Pell of "hindering the acceptance of gay people in 
Australian society" through their religious instruction 
(Wikipedia, 2024). This confrontation highlighted ongoing 
institutional homophobia even after legal decriminalization. 
Most notably, when Kirby led a UN inquiry into North 
Korean human rights abuses, the North Korean state news 
agency KCNA responded with homophobic attacks, 
declaring it "ridiculous for such gay [sic] to sponsor dealing 
with others' human rights issue" (Wikipedia, 2024). This 
international incident demonstrated how sexuality continues 
to be weaponized to discredit political figures. 

5.4 Senator Penny Wong (b. 1968): Highest-Ranking 
LGBTQ+ Politician 
Senator Penelope Ying-Yen Wong has served as 
Australia's Minister for Foreign Affairs since 2022, making 
her one of the most powerful LGBTQ+ women political 
leaders globally (Star Observer, 2022). 
Political Trajectory: 
•First openly lesbian federal parliamentarian (elected 
2002) 
•Senate Leader for the Labor Party 
•First openly LGBTQ+ woman in the Australian Senate 
•Australia's highest-ranking LGBTQ+ and Asian-
Australian politician 
Complex Political Positioning: 
Wong's career illustrates the complex navigation required of 
LGBTQ+ politicians. For years, she opposed same-sex 
marriage publicly while in a same-sex relationship, 
adhering to Labor Party policy despite personal belief. 
Critics argued she "betrayed gay and lesbian Australians" 
by using "culture, religion and history to justify this 
conservative position" (Come The Revolution, 2019). 
When marriage equality finally passed in 2017, Wong's 
emotional response demonstrated the personal toll of 
political compromise: "For Penny Wong, this moment in 
Australian history was personal" (The Guardian, 2017). 
6. The AIDS Crisis: Heightened Persecution 
and Community Response (1980s-1990s) 

6.1 The Crisis Emerges 
When HIV/AIDS emerged in the early 1980s, it rapidly 
became conflated with gay male identity in public 
consciousness. "The AIDS epidemic heightened public 
fears of gay men, while at the same time galvanizing the 
LGBTQ+ community to organize and advocate" (Oxford 
Research Encyclopedia, 2019). 
6.2 Dual Impact: Stigma and Mobilization 
The AIDS crisis had contradictory effects: 
Increased Persecution: 
•Intensified homophobic rhetoric ("gay plague") 
•Employment discrimination against HIV-positive 
individuals 
•Media sensationalism linking homosexuality with 
disease and death 
•Religious condemnation of gay men as receiving 
"divine punishment" 
•Social ostracism and family rejection 
Community Mobilization: 
•Formation of AIDS Council of NSW (ACON) and 
similar organizations 
•Peer education and safe sex campaigns 
•Political advocacy for health funding 
•Creation of support networks and care systems 
•Increased visibility and solidarity within LGBTQ+ 
communities 
6.3 Australia's Public Health Response 

Despite the social stigma, Australia developed one of the 
world's most effective public health responses to HIV/
AIDS, characterized by: 
•Partnership between health authorities and LGBTQ+ 
community organizations 
•Harm reduction approaches (needle exchange 
programs) 
•Non-punitive public health messaging 
•Investment in community-led education campaigns 
This response demonstrated that when LGBTQ+ 
communities are treated as partners rather than pariahs, 
public health outcomes improve dramatically. 
7. Gay Hate Crimes: The Sydney Cliff 
Murders and Police Complicity 
7.1 Systematic Violence at Bondi and Tamarama 
Between approximately 1970 and 2010, a series of murders 
and suspicious deaths of gay men occurred at cliffs and 
coastal areas around Sydney, particularly at Bondi Beach, 
Tamarama, and North Head. Research suggests at least 30 
unsolved deaths may have been gay hate crimes (SBS, 
2016; The Guardian, 2016). 
7.2 Modus Operandi 
Victims were typically: 
•Targeted at known gay cruising areas ("beats") near 
cliffs 

•Attacked by groups of young men (gangs like "The 
Bondi Boys," "Alexandria Eight," "Tamarama Three") 
•Beaten, robbed, and thrown from cliffs 
•Found dead on rocks below or in the ocean 
•Initially dismissed by police as "suicides" or 
"misadventure" 
7.3 Police Failures and Complicity 
The NSW Police Force has admitted officers "may have 
made serious mistakes" while investigating these deaths 
(SBS, 2016). Evidence suggests: 
1.Deliberate misclassification of murders as suicides 
2.Failure to investigate due to homophobic attitudes 
3.Dismissal of witness testimony from LGBTQ+ 
individuals 
4.Destruction of evidence in some cases 
5.Possible police involvement in some attacks (as with 
Dr. Duncan in Adelaide) 
7.4 Operation Taradale and Belated Justice 
Not until 2005-2012 did police conduct systematic reviews 
through Operation Taradale, re-examining dozens of 
suspected gay hate homicides. This investigation led to: 
•Reclassification of many "suicides" as homicides 
•Coroner's inquests reopened 
•Some prosecutions decades after crimes 
•Public acknowledgment of police failures 
7.5 Scott Johnson Case: Family-Driven Justice 
The murder of Scott Johnson, an American mathematician 
whose body was found at the base of North Head cliffs in 

1988, exemplifies the pattern. Initially ruled a suicide, his 
death was only reclassified as a gay hate crime after his 
brother, Steve Johnson, spent decades and millions of 
dollars investigating. 
In January 2022, Scott White confessed to the murder—34 
years after the crime. Steve Johnson stated: "In 2005 I 
became aware of inquests into similar deaths of gay men at 
Bondi Beach in Sydney—the moment when I 'woke up' to 
what really happened" (BBC, 2022). 
7.6 Cultural Documentation 
The systematic violence has been documented in: 
•SBS documentary series "Deep Water" (2016) 
•Podcast "Bondi Badlands"  
•Book Bondi Badlands: The definitive story of Sydney's 
gay hate murders 
•Crime Investigation Australia episode "Bloodsport: 
The Bondi Gay Murders" 
The NSW Police Force characterized these killings as "an 
ugly part of our history" while acknowledging that justice 
has been delayed or denied for most victims (Facebook, 
2024). 
8. Political Scandals and the Weaponization of 
Sexuality 
8.1 The Kernot-Evans Affair (1997-2002) 
One of Australia's most significant political scandals 
involving sexuality was the extramarital relationship 

between Cheryl Kernot (Leader of the Australian 
Democrats) and Gareth Evans (Labor Deputy Leader and 
former Foreign Minister). 
Timeline: 
•1997: Kernot dramatically defected from Democrats to 
Labor Party, shocking Australian politics 
•1997-2000: Relationship with Evans (both married to 
others) 
•2002: Affair revealed publicly, recontextualizing 
Kernot's defection 
Political Ramifications: 
The affair revelation transformed public understanding of 
one of Australian politics' most dramatic defections. Critics 
argued: 
•Kernot's defection was motivated by personal 
relationship rather than policy 
•Labor leadership recruited Kernot while Evans 
conducted affair—possible complicity 
•Betrayal of Democrats voters who supported her 
leadership 
•Double standard in media coverage (intense scrutiny of 
Kernot, less of Evans) 
The scandal demonstrated how sexuality and personal 
relationships, when exposed, can retrospectively alter 
political history. Kernot's political career never recovered; 
Evans retired from frontline politics (SMH, 2002; Crikey, 
2020). 
8.2 George Pell and Catholic Church Sexual Abuse 

While not primarily about consensual sexuality, the 
Cardinal George Pell scandal represents Australia's most 
notorious case involving sexual abuse, institutional cover-
up, and the Catholic Church's protection of predators. 
George Pell (1941-2023): 
•Catholic Cardinal and Vatican treasurer 
•One of the most powerful Catholic officials globally 
•2018: Convicted of child sexual abuse (two choirboys 
in 1996) 
•2020: Conviction overturned by High Court on appeal 
•2017: Royal Commission found Pell knew of abuse 
and failed to act 
Systemic Failures: 
The Royal Commission into Institutional Responses to 
Child Sexual Abuse (2013-2017) found: 
•The Catholic Church had the worst record of any 
institution examined for sexual abuse and cover-ups 
•Over 4,444 incidents of child sexual abuse alleged 
against 1,880 individuals in Catholic institutions 
between 1980-2015 
•Systematic failures to report, investigate, or remove 
abusive clergy 
•Financial settlements prioritized over child protection 
Pell's case, while involving abuse of minors rather than 
consensual adult relationships, illustrates how institutions 
weaponize secrecy around sexuality to protect powerful 
individuals. The Church's treatment of abuse paralleled its 
condemnation of consensual homosexuality, demonstrating 
institutional hypocrisy (The Guardian, 2023; CNN, 2019). 

8.3 Historical Political Scandals and Homosexual 
Accusations 
Throughout Australian political history, accusations of 
homosexuality have been deployed as career-ending 
political weapons: 
19th Century: 
•Victorian-era British colonial scandals involving 
"homosexual perversity" punctuated colonial 
administration 
•Accusations used to remove officials and discredit 
political opponents 
20th Century: 
•Political rivals sometimes deployed insinuations about 
sexuality to undermine opponents 
•Media complicity in "outing" politicians without 
consent 
•Career destruction through sexual orientation exposure 
9. Religious Opposition and Institutional 
Homophobia 
9.1 Archbishop George Pell and Peter Jensen 
In November 2007, Justice Michael Kirby publicly 
confronted religious institutional opposition: 
"Michael Kirby publicly accused the Anglican 
Archbishop of Sydney, Peter Jensen, and 
the Catholic Archbishop of Sydney, George 
Pell, of 'hindering the acceptance of gay 

people in Australian society.' He explicitly 
stated that homophobia was 'reinforced 
even to this day by religious instruction' 
from these prominent religious leaders" 
(Wikipedia, 2024). 
This rare public rebuke from a High Court Justice 
highlighted: 
•Ongoing religious opposition to LGBTQ+ equality 
•The role of religious institutions in perpetuating 
discrimination 
•Political cover that religious authority provides for 
homophobia 
9.2 Conversion Therapy 
Religious institutions in Australia promoted and practiced 
conversion therapy—attempting to change sexual 
orientation through: 
•Psychological manipulation 
•Religious counseling and exorcism 
•Behavioral modification techniques 
•Family pressure and isolation 
Victoria became the first Australian state to ban conversion 
therapy in 2020, with other states following. However, 
religious exemptions often allow practices to continue 
under different labels. 
10. Linguistic Reclamation: From Slurs to 
Identity 
10.1 The Evolution of "Queer" 

The word "queer" exemplifies linguistic reclamation in 
LGBTQ+ history: 
Early History (16th-19th Century): 
•Entered English by early 16th century meaning 
"strange, odd, peculiar or eccentric" 
•By late 19th century, used colloquially for same-sex 
attracted men (La Trobe University, 2023) 
Dual Meanings (Early-Mid 20th Century): 
•Derogatory: "fairies, pansies, and queers conducted 
[...] lewd practices" (1936) 
•Neutral: "opportunity of meeting some of the 
prominent 'queers'" (1914) 
•Affirming: "young men who call themselves 'queers'" 
(1952) 
Gay Liberation Reclamation (1960s-1970s): 
•Activists chanted "out of the closets, into the streets" 
and sang "we're here because we're queer" 
•Deliberate reclamation from hurtful usage 
•Some liberationists wanted to "cancel the word 
homosexual" as limiting 
Australian Terminology: 
•"Camp" was briefly the most common label before 
"gay" became prominent 
•By early 1990s, "gay" referred more typically to gay 
men specifically 
•Evolution to "lesbian and gay," then "LGBT" 
Queer Theory (Early 1990s): 

•"Queer" deployed as critical/political identity 
challenging normative ideas 
•Influenced by Michel Foucault, Judith Butler, Eve 
Kosofsky Sedgwick 
•Activist groups like Queer Nation adopted assertive, 
anti-assimilationist positioning 
Contemporary Usage (2000s-Present): 
•"Queer" as inclusive umbrella term for LGBTIQA+ 
spectrum 
•Recognized in style guides as respectful terminology 
•Some still find it offensive due to traumatic 
associations 
Reclamation Principle: 
"Liberating words from non-affirming religious, 
clinical or colloquial contexts and giving 
them our own meanings is one of the 
defining characteristics of LGBTIQA+ 
history" (La Trobe University, 2023). 
10.2 Other Reclaimed Terms 
Similar reclamation processes have occurred with: 
•"Gay": From slang to primary identity term 
•"Dyke": Reclaimed by some lesbians 
•"Faggot": Some gay men have reclaimed in defiance 
•"Camp": From insult to cultural aesthetic 
The reclamation process is never complete or universal. 
Terms retain traumatic associations for older generations 
who experienced them as violent slurs, while younger 
people may adopt them as empowering identity markers.

This research establishes that the most notorious "cock 
sucker" in Australian history is not an individual but
