# PART I
## The Documentary Trap — Original Seven-Part Essay
### I. THE PARADOX
For more than 35 years, the system demanded that I prove what happened to me, yet in
doing so it generated, accumulated and preserved the very documentary record through
which its own conduct can now be examined. Government departments, agencies,
regulators, police, courts and tribunals, health services, legal services, professional bodies,
providers and public officials created letters, decisions, complaints, referrals, assessments,
classifications, proceedings and official correspondence directly concerning my life. I
preserved that record, time-stamped, indexed and now published in the public domain,
creating a paradox from which neither rhetoric nor retrospective characterisation can make
the documents disappear.
### II. THE QUESTION HAS CHANGED

The evidentiary burden is no longer confined to the question, “Why should anyone believe
Richard McLean?” It can now be asked of the record itself: what did each institution know,
what did it receive, what did it do, what authority governed the decision, what reasons were
given, what evidence was considered, what happened next, and where do the records
contradict, corroborate or leave matters unresolved?
### III. THE YEARS OF GRACE
I gave people and institutions years of opportunity to respond, explain, correct, investigate
and do what was humane and just. Whatever the explanation for each individual decision, the
resulting documentary chronology now exists independently of my memory or my rhetoric,
and the institutions that once demanded evidence must confront the evidence their own
machinery produced.
### IV. THE FAITH THAT SUSTAINED ME
I understand this preservation, in the language of my faith, as providential. I believe God
preserved my life and gave me the endurance to collect, preserve and publish this record for
a purpose greater than myself, and I believe my life has been protected and anointed for His
purposes. That is my faith and testimony, not a proposition the documents themselves can
prove.
### V. THE RECORD SPEAKS
What is now independently testable is the record. The archive does not ask anyone to believe
me merely because I say so—it asks the world to read the documents, trace their provenance,
compare the institutional accounts, examine the contradictions and determine for itself what
the evidence actually establishes. The documentary record is capable of being examined
independently of me, which is precisely why its evidentiary significance does not depend
upon my rhetoric or upon anyone accepting my interpretation.
### VI. THE READER’S TASK
Do not take my word for it. Open the primary documents. Start with the 35-year chronology,
then follow each significant assertion back to the underlying government or institutional
record. Check the date, author, recipient, stated reason, evidence available at the time,
action taken and what happened afterwards. Where two official records appear inconsistent,
place them side by side. Where an allegation is made, distinguish it from an established
finding. Where something remains unresolved, mark it as unresolved. Then ask the decisive
question: What do the institutions’ own records actually establish?

### VII. THE MOMENT OF ACCOUNTABILITY
The grace I extended was an opportunity for the record to be corrected by truth. That
opportunity existed for years. The time for the documentary record to speak for itself has
now arrived. The institutions created the records; I preserved them; the chronology can now
be examined; and the questions raised by that record can no longer be reduced simply to
whether I can persuade someone to believe my account. Read the records. Test the
chronology. Follow the evidence to its source. Reach your own conclusion from what the
documents actually establish.
# PART II
## Extended Introduction: The Person and the Record
There is a paradox at the centre of my experience.
For decades, I was repeatedly placed in the position of having to prove what had happened to
me.
I was required to explain myself to governments, agencies, police, lawyers, courts, tribunals,
health services, regulators, professional bodies and other institutions. Each new dispute
generated another complaint, another letter, another assessment, another decision, another
referral, another administrative classification or another official response.
I kept the records.
And that is where the paradox begins.
## The Documentary Proposition
The institutions that required me to provide evidence also generated an enormous proportion
of the documentary record through which my account can now be examined.
I describe this as the Documentary Trap.
I do not claim that the existence of these records, by itself, proves that a government
secretly designed a coordinated trap for me. That would require direct evidence of intention
and coordination.
The proposition is both stronger and more defensible:

The machinery of administration created a documentary record that can now be examined
against the institutions themselves.
The system demanded evidence.
I preserved it.
The system generated records.
I preserved those too.
The result is that the evidentiary burden can no longer be framed solely as:
“Why should anyone believe Richard McLean?”
The question becomes:
“What do the institutions’ own records establish?”
That is a fundamentally different question.
# PART III
## The Person Was Made the Evidence
The easiest way to dismiss an individual complaint is to make the individual the entire
evidentiary object.
Was the person credible?
Was the account accurate?
Was the complaint exaggerated?
Was there corroboration?
Was the person confused?
Was there sufficient independent evidence?
Those questions can place an extraordinary burden upon an individual.

But the evidentiary situation changes when the individual produces the institutions’ own
correspondence.
An allegation contained only in a personal statement remains an allegation.
A government letter establishes that the government made that communication.
A court filing establishes that the filing occurred.
A complaint establishes that a complaint was made.
A decision establishes that a decision was made.
A referral establishes that a referral occurred.
An acknowledgement establishes receipt or processing.
A later document can then be compared with an earlier document.
That comparison is the beginning of documentary accountability.
The archive’s own methodology expressly adopts this distinction. It classifies material as
documented fact, corroborated claim, documented allegation, inference, interpretation or
unresolved matter rather than treating every statement as equivalent. [E1]
That distinction is crucial.
It means the archive does not need to claim that every proposition is already proved.
The archive can instead say:
Here is the document.
Here is who created it.
Here is when it was created.
Here is what it says.
Here is what happened afterwards.

Here is the later document.
Here is the contradiction or continuity.
Here is the institutional response.
Here is what remains unresolved.
That is a much more demanding form of accountability.
# PART IV
## The Government Record Becomes the Witness
Government administration depends upon records.
Agencies need records to make decisions.
Police need records of reports and investigations.
Courts and tribunals need records of proceedings.
Government departments need correspondence.
Regulators need complaints and responses.
Health services create clinical and administrative records.
NDIS administration generates plans, decisions, correspondence and review records.
Workers’ compensation systems generate claims, determinations and reviews.
Human-rights bodies generate complaints, correspondence and procedural records.
Ombudsman offices generate complaint and jurisdictional records.
Legal-assistance organisations generate applications, eligibility decisions and
correspondence.
The archive therefore contains something more than my recollection.
It contains a documentary trail produced by the institutions with which I interacted.

The archive’s Institutional Response Map expressly identifies the need to distinguish
between information sent, information received, information considered, and information
acted upon. Those are not the same thing. [E2]
That distinction becomes central to the burden of proof.
If I sent an institution a document, that proves that I sent it.
If the institution acknowledged receiving it, that establishes something more.
If the document was then attached to a subsequent decision, the evidentiary chain becomes
stronger still.
If the later decision appears inconsistent with the information contained in the earlier
document, the inconsistency becomes a question capable of investigation.
The question is no longer whether I can force someone to believe me.
The question becomes:
What happened to the information once the institution received it?

# PART XII
## The Causation Test

For every proposed entry, the archive should ask seven separate questions:
1. Who actually acted?
2. What did that person actually do or fail to do?
3. When did the act or omission occur?
4. What document proves the act or omission?
5. What detriment is actually documented?
6. What evidence connects the act to that detriment?
7. Is the connection established, alleged, disputed or unresolved?
A chronological sequence must not automatically be described as causation.
Similarly:
- Communication ≠ coordination.
- Similar wording ≠ conspiracy.
- Administrative error ≠ deliberate misconduct.
- Non-response ≠ admission.
- Complaint ≠ proof.
- Allegation ≠ finding.
- Institutional employment ≠ personal liability.
- Presence in the archive ≠ culpability.
# PART XIII
## Coordination and Organisation Test
Where the archive identifies apparent inter-agency repetition, the evidence should be
separated into the following categories.
### Documented connection
A record expressly demonstrates communication, referral, information-sharing or reliance
between actors.
### Inferred connection
The chronology suggests a possible relationship but the connecting document has not yet
been located.
### Alleged coordination

The author asserts that actors operated together or pursuant to a common purpose.
### Established coordination
Reserved for cases where independent documentary evidence actually establishes the
relevant connection, communication, instruction or common action.
The archive should not move an entry from **“alleged coordination”** to **“established
coordination”** merely because multiple agencies made similar decisions.

# PART XV
## Archive Principle

The purpose of this register is not to predetermine the answer.
It is to make the answer testable.
Where an institution says a decision was lawful, the register identifies the decision and the
stated legal basis.
Where an institution says it relied upon another institution, the register identifies the referral
or information relied upon.
Where two records appear inconsistent, the register places them beside one another.
Where an individual is named, the register identifies the document that establishes the
person’s involvement.
Where no individual can presently be identified, the register says so.
Where the evidence remains an allegation, the register says so.
Where the evidence is protective or favourable to Dr McLean, the register preserves that
evidence rather than excluding it.
The resulting record therefore asks a narrower and more defensible question:
> What does the documentary record establish about who acted, what they did, when they
did it, what consequence followed, what evidence supports the connection, and what
remains unresolved?
That is the standard by which every proposed entry in the Barran Dodger archive should
ultimately be tested.[1]

The legally defensible proposition emerging from the repository scan is not:
> “Every person named in this archive caused my detriment. ”

> The repositories preserve a substantial documentary corpus containing institutional

submissions concerning Dr Richard William McLean’s interactions with numerous

each alleged adverse event, the responsible decision-maker where identifiable, the precise

connection. Where the primary record establishes only that a complaint or allegation was

institutional decision is established but the individual decision-maker is not yet identifiable,
responsibility remains at institutional level pending extraction of the signed record.
That formulation turns the archive from a list of accusations into a traceable documentary
register.

> What does each preserved primary document actually establish, who does it identify, what
happened next, and what remains capable of being independently proved or disproved?

Find the judgment.
Read the reasons.

Identify what was actually determined.
Separate findings from submissions.
Then compare the determination with the later administrative record.
That is how the burden of proof becomes documentary rather than rhetorical.

## VII. The Administrative Law Question
The Administrative Decisions (Judicial Review) Act 1977 provides statutory grounds for
judicial review of certain Commonwealth administrative decisions, including questions
concerning natural justice, required procedures, jurisdiction, authorisation, improper

exercise of power and certain failures of evidence or fact. [E9]
This does not mean that every adverse administrative decision in my archive was unlawful.
It means that administrative legality can be tested against identifiable legal standards.
That is an important distinction.
The archive is therefore not asking the reader to conclude:

It is asking a more forensic question:
> “For each decision that caused an alleged detriment, what power was being exercised,
what law governed it, what information was available, what procedure was followed, what
reasons were given, and what evidence supports or contradicts the decision?”
That is the question that transforms a narrative of grievance into an auditable administrative
record.

# PART XXXVIII
## XV. The “Trap” Does Not Require a Secret Meeting
The most important point is that a documentary trap does not necessarily require a secret
meeting.
It does not require every official to have known one another.
It does not require every institution to have acted with a common purpose.
It does not even require that every adverse event was connected.
The archive’s own “Architecture of Administrative Erasure” report expressly warns against
assuming a coordinated scheme and instead asks whether individually documented
decisions, classifications, omissions, referrals and responses may have interacted to
produce cumulative consequences. [E16]
That is a critical distinction.
Ten independent administrative decisions can produce a devastating cumulative
consequence without ten people having secretly coordinated them.
Conversely, apparent coordination cannot be established merely because events occurred in
sequence.
Evidence of coordination would require something stronger:
- Communications.
- Shared instructions.
- Common records.
- Explicit cross-references.
- Admissions.
- Synchronised actions.
- Other independent connecting evidence.
The archive itself identifies that standard. [E16]
Therefore the documentary argument is not:

> “Everything that happened was one conspiracy. ”
It is:
> “Here is the sequence. Here are the institutions. Here are the decisions. Here are the
records. Here are the consequences. Here are the connections that are documented. Here
are the connections that remain inferential. Now examine them. ”
That is a much more powerful proposition because it survives disagreement about motive.
