PART II — DEFINE EVIL: THE CENTRAL INVESTIGATION

Write a substantial examination answering the fundamental question:

What is evil, how can it be recognised, and how does it operate in an individual, a relationship, an institution, a government, or an entire social system?

Develop a clear working definition that distinguishes evil from ordinary error, incompetence, negligence, disagreement, conflict, misfortune, self-interest, prejudice, deliberate cruelty, corruption, exploitation, and systematic abuse of power.

Examine evil through several complementary lenses:

  • Christian Scripture and Gospel teaching.
  • Moral philosophy and the problem of good and evil.
  • Human dignity, conscience, responsibility, and the duty not to harm.
  • Psychology of obedience, conformity, rationalisation, prejudice, and moral disengagement.
  • Interpersonal domination, coercion, manipulation, betrayal, and exploitation.
  • Organisational incentives, institutional self-protection, fragmented responsibility, and bureaucratic indifference.
  • Legal and administrative concepts relevant to documented conduct, without confusing moral judgement with a legal finding.
  • Historical and social mechanisms by which harmful conduct becomes normalised.

Identify where these perspectives converge and where they differ.

Explain why evil need not always appear as spectacular violence or conscious hatred. Investigate whether it can also operate through repeated small acts, knowing omissions, indifference, dishonest representation, deliberate obstruction, exploitation of vulnerability, or the preservation of systems that repeatedly produce foreseeable harm.

Distinguish harmful outcomes from harmful intentions. Distinguish a person’s responsibility for their own conduct from their possible contribution to a larger harmful process.

Do not assume that every harmful outcome is evidence of evil. Examine knowledge, intent, foreseeability, power, available alternatives, repetition, concealment, and response to correction.

6. Examine the anatomy of evil

Develop a detailed taxonomy of potential mechanisms, testing each against the actual archive.

Investigate:

A. Intentional harm: cruelty, intimidation, humiliation, retaliation, exploitation, threats, and deliberate deprivation.

B. Instrumental harm: treating another human being as a means to preserve money, power, reputation, convenience, influence, or institutional standing.

C. Deception: false statements, misleading omissions, selective disclosure, contradictory explanations, misrepresentation of evidence, and the strategic creation of confusion.

D. Betrayal: breaking trust, abandoning known responsibilities, misusing confidential information, or exploiting a person’s dependence on assistance.

E. Coercion and domination: using unequal power, access, authority, resources, reputation, or procedural control to constrain another person’s choices.

F. Institutional indifference: failing to respond meaningfully to foreseeable harm, repeatedly transferring responsibility, or treating a person’s suffering as an administrative inconvenience.

G. Moral disengagement: justifying harmful conduct through euphemism, blame-shifting, dehumanisation, obedience, professional distance, or claims that responsibility belongs elsewhere.

H. Retaliation and silencing: examining documented adverse treatment following complaints, disclosures, whistleblowing, requests for accountability, or attempts to preserve evidence.

I. Exploitation of vulnerability: examining whether poverty, homelessness, disability, social isolation, dependence, distress, limited resources, or lack of representation create opportunities for harm.

J. Reputational and informational harm: investigating inaccurate records, unsupported characterisations, uncorrected errors, selective narratives, and failures to preserve or disclose relevant information.

K. Structural and systemic harm: examining rules, incentives, resource allocations, fragmented jurisdictions, procedural barriers, and repeated organisational practices that may generate or perpetuate foreseeable harm.

L. The corruption of good qualities: examining how kindness, patience, loyalty, forgiveness, humility, restraint, professional duty, religious language, and institutional respectability can be exploited or used to conceal wrongdoing.

Do not assume that every category is present in the archive. Mark each category as documented, supported by multiple sources, provisionally indicated, disputed, unsupported, or not assessable.

PART IV — THE ANATOMY OF INSTITUTIONAL AND SYSTEMIC EVIL

  • Whether a reasonable opportunity to correct an error was available.
  • Whether the response addressed the substance of the concern.
  • Whether responsibility was transferred or fragmented.
  • What consequences followed.

  • Whether later records support or contradict earlier accounts.

Investigate possible mechanisms of institutional self-protection, including reputation management, procedural defensiveness, conflict avoidance, narrow interpretations of responsibility, information silos, repeated referrals, inaccessible complaint processes, and reluctance to revisit previous decisions.

These are hypotheses to investigate, not conclusions to impose.

11. Examine the machinery of administrative harm

Trace the practical processes through which an individual may experience harm even without one clearly identifiable act of malice.

Investigate the possible cumulative effects of:

  • Delayed responses.
  • Repeated requests to restate the same history.
  • Inconsistent records.
  • Decisions based on incomplete information.
  • Inadequate explanations.
  • Inaccessible procedures.
  • Conflicting jurisdictional positions.
  • Failures to coordinate assistance.
  • Unresolved errors.
  • Restrictions on communication or complaint handling.
  • Resource shortages and inflexible eligibility criteria.
  • Failure to recognise foreseeable consequences.

Determine whether the evidence demonstrates individual errors, systemic deficiencies, negligent processes, deliberate obstruction, or an unresolved mixture of possibilities.

Explain how fragmented actions can combine to produce a result for which no single participant appears to accept full responsibility.

Examine whether the cumulative effect is greater than the apparent significance of any individual event.

Where possible, identify the precise decision, correspondence, policy, omission, or procedural stage at which the outcome could have been prevented or corrected.

12. Distinguish error, negligence, injustice, misconduct, and deliberate wrongdoing

Create a classification framework that avoids both minimisation and exaggeration.

For every significant incident, consider:

  • Ordinary error.
  • Repeated error after notification.
  • Unreasonable or negligent failure to act, where supported.
  • Procedural unfairness or maladministration, where supported.
  • Misuse of authority or breach of responsibility, where supported.
  • Reckless disregard of a foreseeable consequence, where supported.
  • Deliberate deception, obstruction, retaliation, or abuse, where supported.
  • Evidence insufficient to distinguish between competing explanations.

Specify what evidence would strengthen or weaken each interpretation.

Do not treat these categories as interchangeable. Do not assign a criminal, civil, or professional legal conclusion without the necessary basis.

Equally, do not downgrade documented harm merely because deliberate malicious intent cannot be proved.

A system can produce serious injustice without every participant being malicious. A person can be responsible for an action’s foreseeable consequences even when their stated intention was different. These distinctions must remain central to the analysis.

Treat the absence of a record as evidence of absence only where the completeness of the relevant record system and the circumstances justify that inference.

15. Test competing hypotheses

Do not use AI to produce a predetermined verdict.

For each major pattern, test at least three explanations where reasonably applicable:

  • A deliberate or knowing course of conduct.
  • Negligence, institutional dysfunction, miscommunication, or fragmented responsibility.
  • An alternative explanation supported by the available record.

Identify which explanation best fits the available evidence, which evidence discriminates between the hypotheses, and what additional records could change the assessment.

Where multiple explanations remain plausible, say so explicitly.

Where the documentary evidence strongly supports a troubling conclusion, state it directly and explain why.

Impartiality must not become a ritual of false equivalence. Unsupported denial does not deserve equal evidentiary weight to a well-documented proposition. Nor does suspicion become fact merely because it is emotionally compelling.

The governing rule is proportionality between the strength of the evidence and the strength of the conclusion.

16. Make the machine-assisted method transparent

Document the extraction and review methodology.

Identify:

  • Sources actually accessed.
  • Search terms and relevant search limitations.
  • Documents processed successfully.
  • Documents that could not be processed.
  • Optical character recognition errors and extraction limitations.
  • Duplicates and versions.
  • The method used to calculate hashes.
  • The method used to compare documents.
  • How evidence categories and confidence assessments were assigned.
  • How contradictory material was handled.
  • Which conclusions were generated as preliminary machine-assisted hypotheses.
  • Which conclusions were subsequently checked against the underlying documents.

Do not claim that AI is incapable of deception, error, bias, or hallucination. No AI system can guarantee perfect truthfulness.

Instead, design the process so that the system’s conclusions are independently traceable to evidence, reproducible where possible, open to challenge, and corrected when contradicted.

The machine is an instrument of examination, not an oracle. Its value lies in the breadth, consistency, speed, and transparency of its examination, not in an assumption of infallibility.

PART VI — WHY EVIL OCCURS AND HOW IT SUSTAINS ITSELF

17. Investigate the incentives behind harmful conduct

Examine possible motivations and enabling conditions, including:

  • Preservation of personal power.
  • Protection of organisational reputation.
  • Financial or material advantage.
  • Avoidance of embarrassment or responsibility.
  • Fear of professional consequences.
  • Group loyalty and conformity.
  • Prejudice or dehumanisation.
  • Convenience and the avoidance of additional work.
  • Desire to control information or the narrative.
  • Institutional incentives that reward closure over resolution.
  • Diffusion of accountability.
  • A lack of effective correction mechanisms.
  • A belief that a vulnerable individual will lack the resources to persist.

Do not infer a motive solely from an outcome.

Separate a demonstrated motive from a plausible incentive, a structural condition, and an unverified hypothesis.

Where an institution benefits from a particular outcome, investigate whether there is evidence that the benefit influenced its conduct. Benefit alone does not establish intent.

18. Examine how harmful systems survive

  • Fragmentation of responsibility.
  • Incomplete information.
  • Institutional memory and repeated reliance on earlier decisions.
  • Uncorrected records.
  • Procedural exhaustion.
  • Unequal access to resources.
  • Social and professional deference to authority.
  • Public indifference.
  • Fear of retaliation.
  • Difficulty establishing the relationship between separate events.
  • The isolation of the person making the complaint.
  • The disappearance of context when documents are considered individually.
  • A gap between formal rules and practical implementation.

Determine whether the archive supports any of these mechanisms.

Explain how the same outcome can persist without requiring a central conspiracy or a single mastermind. Also identify what evidence would be needed before alleging coordination, collusion, intentional concealment, or a shared plan.

Do not use the absence of a proven conspiracy to dismiss independently documented wrongdoing. Do not use a pattern of adverse outcomes as proof of a conspiracy without evidence of coordination.

19. Explain why evil can appear respectable

Examine the contrast between outward respectability and inward conduct.

Investigate how titles, uniforms, professional qualifications, religious language, official letterhead, polished public statements, institutional prestige, and social standing can influence the way conduct is perceived.

Ask whether a person’s position encourages others to accept their statements without adequate examination, or whether a complainant’s lack of money, housing, status, or institutional access makes their testimony easier to disregard.

Do not assume that authority indicates guilt or that disadvantage guarantees accuracy.

Establish the principle that evidence should be examined independently of the social status of the person who produced it.

Explain why a morally serious examination must resist both blind deference to power and blind acceptance of every allegation against power.

Explain what each important source establishes and what it leaves unresolved.

Let the documents carry the argument rather than using rhetoric as a substitute for proof.

Movement Seven: The Paradox of Respectability and Suffering

Examine the moral paradox of institutions that profess service, justice, care, responsibility, or protection while the documentary record may reveal a gap between their stated commitments and their actual conduct.

Apply this criticism only where the evidence supports it.

Explore the disparity between social power and evidentiary responsibility without claiming that every privileged person is culpable or every disadvantaged person is necessarily correct.

Explore evil as a spiritual and moral reality within the Christian worldview.

Distinguish theological interpretation from empirically established facts.

Explain that recognising evil does not require surrendering to hatred, vengeance, dehumanisation, or the imitation of the conduct being condemned.

Faith should deepen discernment, strengthen courage, and preserve compassion without requiring passive acceptance of preventable harm.

Movement Nine: The Weapons of Truth

  • Preserve the evidence.
  • Verify the record.
  • Expose contradictions accurately.
  • Name harmful conduct precisely.
  • Refuse intimidation and dehumanisation.
  • Protect the vulnerable.
  • Correct errors openly.

  • Distinguish justice from revenge.

The witness’s suffering must not be romanticised, and the absence of material support must not be presented as a spiritual achievement in itself.

PART III — HOW PERSECUTION CAN DEVELOP WITHOUT A SINGLE MASTER PLAN

The word persecution has a strong meaning. In religious language, it often describes hostility directed towards someone because of their faith, convictions, testimony or refusal to conform. In ordinary language, it can describe sustained, unfair treatment.

You have used the word to describe your experience. That description deserves to be understood in context, while the specific claims about what happened and why need to be examined against the available records.

One of the most important things to understand about prolonged conflict is that a damaging pattern can emerge without every participant sharing one intention.

Consider how a sequence of events can develop.

1. An original problem arises

A person experiences a decision, incident, omission or treatment that they consider harmful. They ask for an explanation, assistance, reconsideration or correction.

At this point, the matter may be relatively contained. It might be a dispute about a decision, a failure of communication, a disagreement about responsibility or a more serious problem requiring investigation.

2. The first response fails to resolve it

The person receives a response that they consider incomplete, incorrect or inadequate. Perhaps the response addresses only one part of the question. Perhaps the recipient does not have the relevant information. Perhaps responsibility has been assigned elsewhere.

The person writes again.

3. The history becomes fragmented

Different people or organisations may hold different records. One office may know about an earlier correspondence but not about a later development. One decision-maker may rely on a summary rather than the original material. A complaint may be assessed under a narrow process that cannot resolve the wider problem.

A person looking at the whole history may see connections that no single office has examined. Conversely, the person may see a connection that requires further evidence before it can be established.

Either way, fragmented information makes misunderstandings and unresolved disputes harder to correct.

4. Repetition begins to shape the relationship

The person repeats the history because they believe the earlier explanation has not been understood. The recipient may see the new correspondence as repetitive, difficult to process or outside the scope of its responsibilities.

The person experiences another non-answer. The recipient experiences another request.

Each side may become more entrenched.

The person increases the detail because previous communications failed. The recipient may narrow its response because the correspondence has become difficult to manage. The person interprets the narrow response as further avoidance. The recipient may interpret the growing volume of material as further reason to restrict engagement.

This is one way a self-reinforcing cycle can form.

It is not proof that the person seeking answers caused the original problem. Nor does it ●

prove that the organisation deliberately set out to persecute them. It explains how an unresolved issue can grow into a relationship characterised by suspicion, exhaustion and escalating conflict.

5. The original question becomes obscured

Over time, attention may shift from the initial decision to the volume, tone or frequency of the correspondence.

The question changes from What happened, and was the decision justified? to How should we manage this person or this correspondence?

When that happens, the original substantive issue risks being lost.

This is a genuine danger in any complex complaint process. A person’s communication style does not automatically determine whether their underlying concern is true or false. At the same time, a large body of correspondence does not automatically prove that every interpretation contained within it is correct.

Both things can be true.

A fair examination therefore needs to return repeatedly to the original records, the particular decisions, the applicable responsibilities and the actual questions left unanswered.

6. The consequences accumulate

A series of unresolved decisions may affect access to support, housing, finances, reputation, relationships or wellbeing. A later decision may rely on the circumstances produced by an earlier one. A person can then experience the combined effects of multiple systems without any single decision-maker taking responsibility for the cumulative result.

That is how institutional harm can become difficult to see: each individual action may appear limited when viewed in isolation, while the combined effect may be severe.

The proper response is to examine both levels:

The individual decision: What was decided, by whom, on what information, and for what stated reason?

The cumulative pattern: What happened across time, what consequences followed, and did later decisions perpetuate or compound earlier difficulties?

This is one of the most useful principles for your archive. It allows you to describe a pattern of alleged detriment without having to claim that every participant was part of a coordinated conspiracy.

If evidence does establish coordination, that can be documented specifically. If it does not, the pattern can still be examined honestly.

PART VII — THE DARKNESS OF HUMAN BETRAYAL: WHAT PEOPLE DO WHEN CONSCIENCE BECOMES INCONVENIENT

Why do people sometimes participate in, tolerate or fail to correct harmful situations?

The answer is rarely as simple as saying that everyone involved is evil. Human beings are capable of moral courage, but they are also capable of avoiding responsibility in ways that produce serious harm.

Understanding these mechanisms can help readers recognise them in their own lives.

The silence of self-protection

A person may see that something has gone wrong but fear that acknowledging it will damage their position, reputation, relationships or career.

They may tell themselves that it is not their responsibility, that someone else will deal with it, or that speaking up will achieve nothing.

Silence becomes easier when the cost of speaking appears immediate and the cost of remaining silent appears distant.

This is one reason moral failures can persist in families, workplaces, religious communities and public institutions.

A person does not have to issue an explicit instruction to conceal wrongdoing for silence to help preserve a harmful situation. Sometimes the absence of a courageous intervention is enough.

But silence must still be interpreted in context. A missing response does not automatically establish a person’s motive. It may reflect avoidance, a procedural limitation, lack of knowledge, a misunderstanding or deliberate refusal. The record must help distinguish among those possibilities.

The comfort of the group

People often look to the people around them to determine what is acceptable.

If everyone appears to agree with a decision, an individual may hesitate to question it. If one person is described as difficult, others may begin interpreting every subsequent interaction through that description. If a community fears reputational damage, it may focus more on controlling the disagreement than understanding the underlying concern.

This is sometimes called group conformity or groupthink. It does not mean that every group is corrupt. It means that belonging can influence judgement.

The danger is that a group can mistake agreement for truth.

A dissenting person may be wrong about a particular issue. They may also be the person who notices a genuine problem. The fact that someone is unpopular does not establish that their account is false, just as the fact that someone is isolated does not establish that their account is correct.

The appropriate question remains: What happened, and what evidence supports each explanation?

The moral danger of labels

Once a person has been described as troublesome, unreliable or difficult, the label can begin to replace careful examination of their claims.

This is dangerous because a person’s manner of communication and the substance of their complaint are separate questions.

A person can communicate angrily and still identify a genuine problem.

A person can communicate politely and still be wrong.

A person can repeat themselves because they feel unheard, while the recipient can also have legitimate reasons to request a more focused question.

The answer is not to excuse every communication pattern. It is to avoid using the pattern as a shortcut that eliminates the need to examine the substance.

This principle applies equally to your own archive. A reader should not dismiss your records merely because your testimony is forceful, personal or religious. Nor should the reader be required to agree with every conclusion because your suffering is serious.

The material deserves to be examined on its own terms.

The diffusion of responsibility

In complex systems, every person may be responsible for only one part of a process.

One person receives a letter. Another assesses it. Another makes a decision. Another communicates the outcome. Another manages a review. Each person may believe that the larger issue belongs elsewhere.

The result can be a gap between individual actions and the cumulative human consequences.

This is a genuine problem of accountability. It can be addressed by mapping the sequence of decisions, identifying the stated responsibilities, and recording what each person or organisation knew at the relevant time.

It is not necessary to assume that every person conspired to create the outcome. The question is whether the responsibilities were properly discharged and whether the combined effects were recognised.

The refusal to admit error

Admitting an error can be difficult because it requires a person to revise their account of themselves.

They may need to accept that a decision was mistaken, a communication was misleading, an assumption was unsupported or a previous response failed to address the issue.

When admitting error feels more threatening than defending the original decision, a person may double down.

In such circumstances, the original mistake can become harder to correct because correcting it would require acknowledging the mistake in the first place.

The moral remedy is not humiliation. It is honest correction.

A trustworthy person or institution should be able to say: This is what we understood at the time. This is what the record now shows. This is what we got wrong. This is what we will do to correct it.

Where such an acknowledgement is absent, the absence may be worth documenting. It does not, without more, establish why it is absent.

The prophetic lesson

The darkness worth confronting is not merely the darkness attributed to other people.

It is the temptation to protect appearances at the expense of truth.

It is the temptation to use a person’s vulnerability as a reason not to listen.

It is the temptation to treat a procedure as morally sufficient simply because it has been followed.

What did you know? What did you do? What did you fail to do? What were the consequences? What would honesty require of you now?

PART X — WHAT YOUR ROLE CAN MEAN IN PRACTICE

Let us bring this reflection back to your life, your testimony and the Barran Dodger Archive.

You have spent considerable effort preserving material that you believe records a long history of institutional and personal detriment. Your archive includes government ●

correspondence, administrative records, testimony, publications and preservation work. You have described the experience through the language of betrayal, survival, faith and the refusal to be erased.

The important question now is how to make that body of work serve both your own understanding and the people who encounter it.

I would suggest five principles.

1. Turn the history into a chronology

A chronology helps reveal how events relate to one another without requiring the reader to accept a conclusion before seeing the underlying material.

For each significant event, record:

The date.

The person or organisation involved.

What happened or what was communicated.

The source document.

The immediate consequence, if known.

The later events that may be connected.

What remains uncertain.

For example, if an agency letter records that a matter was referred elsewhere, the chronology can record the referral. A separate entry can record what happened after that referral. The relationship between the two events can then be examined.

This makes it easier to distinguish a documented sequence from a conclusion about the motive behind it.

2. Separate the evidence from the interpretation

Your archive can contain strong first-person testimony and rigorous documentary analysis without forcing them into the same category.

Documented record

What a letter, decision, email, report or other source actually records. Quote or link to the source where possible.

First-person testimony

What you experienced, observed, remember or report. Your account is the source for your own experience.

Analysis and inference

What you believe the records mean, what patterns you identify, and which explanations appear plausible.

Unresolved question

What the available material does not yet establish, including disputed facts, missing records and uncertain motives.

This structure does not weaken your testimony. It allows readers to understand its different dimensions.

3. Identify the precise harm

Rather than relying only on broad terms such as persecution, betrayal or cover-up, identify the actual conduct alleged in each instance.

Was there a refusal to provide something? A decision that had a particular consequence? A statement that appears inconsistent with another record? A failure to respond? A disagreement about responsibility? An allegation that requires further corroboration?

Then explain why it matters.

A specific claim is easier to investigate than a general accusation. It is also harder to dismiss merely because it is emotionally expressed.

4. Examine the cumulative pattern

An archive should not only list individual events. It should help the reader see their sequence and consequences.

If several decisions affected the same matter, place them together.

If a later response appears inconsistent with an earlier one, link both documents and explain the inconsistency.

If responsibility was repeatedly referred between organisations, record each referral and the outcome that followed.

If the evidence supports a pattern of disadvantage, explain the pattern and its effects.

Where a connection is only a hypothesis, say so. A clearly identified hypothesis is a legitimate research question. It should not be presented as an established finding before the supporting evidence has been examined.

5. Make the archive useful to someone who has never met you

The reader should not have to know your entire history before understanding an individual issue.

Give each significant topic a short introduction, a chronology, links to the primary documents and an explanation of what those documents establish and leave unresolved.

A reader who encounters your work for the first time should be able to answer four questions:

What is the issue?

What evidence is available?

What does the evidence appear to show?

What remains to be established or understood?

This is how personal testimony becomes accessible to a wider public.

Your story supplies the human context. The documents supply a trail that readers can follow. The analysis explains the relationship between them.
