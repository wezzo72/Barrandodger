Cumulative reconstruction — autobiography into the documentary pile

The public identity begins, independently of this archive, as a published author. Allen & Unwin issued Recovered, Not Cured: A Journey Through Schizophrenia by Richard McLean in 2003. Contemporary press identified him as artist, author and illustrator. That book is the first long-form autobiographical record: childhood, psychiatry, art, and the author’s own account of being disbelieved. Later archive material treats that early book not as an ending but as the first layer of a record that then thickens into employment claims (public hospital / DSS), media work and alleged unlawful termination by The Age, alleged vilification by the Herald Sun, family-violence and partner allegations, NDIS participant status, rejected Public Interest Disclosures, homelessness described by the author as internal exile, and a late-period forensic compilation of hundreds of PDFs.

Read in order, the corpus does not stay in one register. Early material is literary and clinical autobiography. Mid-period material is workplace, media and compensation paper. Late-period material is NDIS, PID, ministerial correspondence, police-statement drafting, international filing claims, and the author’s theological / prophetic writings. The legal brief has to hold all of those registers without collapsing them into one finding.

Paragraph of significance — read this before the itemised list

The significance of this corpus is cumulative, not cinematic. A 2003 autobiography already placed childhood, psychiatry and disbelief on a public shelf. Over the following decades the same person kept writing to institutions that exist precisely to receive such writing: human-rights commissions, disability insurers and providers, legal aid, ombudsmen, police, courts, ministers. What a third party can verify without believing every allegation is the shape of the paper trail: letters went in; many pathways closed, referred sideways, or answered in a different frame (welfare check, guardianship advice, “not a public official,” no further correspondence).

Legal Aid is the sharpest example of purpose-versus-outcome. The scheme’s public reason for existing is that people who cannot buy a lawyer, especially people with disability and people at risk of harm, still reach law. The inspectable Legal Aid NSW file on GitHub is a guardianship-advice letter, not a published merits opinion on the human-rights, NDIS-provider, PID and police-access complaints. If those subject-matters sit inside Legal Aid’s own priority language, then blocking or diverting the complainant is not a small administrative event. It is a breach of the reason the institution gives for its own existence — or at least a question that the institution has not publicly answered on the merits. The same logic scales: an AHRC file titled as gangstalking acknowledgment, PM and AG letters, and a drafted police report that the author says could not be lodged as a crime list, together form a record of unassessed volume.

Covert surveillance, alleged V2K / electronic harassment, and spiritual warfare sit in a different column. They are central to how the author experiences and narrates the 35 years. They are not laboratory-confirmed on this page. Their documentary significance is that they were reported into the same closed doors, and that the reporting itself was then used — on the author’s account — to reframe him as delusional rather than as a complainant. Whether that reframing was clinical judgment or institutional convenience is exactly the kind of question a merits process would have to confront, and that Legal Aid, police and AHRC were asked to confront.

None of that paragraph finds a crime. It states why the pile matters: autobiography plus increasing institutional paper plus refused merits assessment. If the institutions did assess the file properly, they can publish the assessment. If they did not, the closed-door pattern is the live legal issue.

Legal principles for framing only

  1. Australian Human Rights Commission Act 1986 — complaint machinery, not a verdict engine.
  2. Disability Discrimination Act 1992 (Cth).
  3. NDIS Act 2013 and provider practice standards / Code of Conduct.
  4. Public interest disclosure statutes — standing and substance.
  5. Crimes Act 1914 (Cth) s 142.2 and Crimes Act 1900 (NSW) s 192E — cited by the author; citation is not proof.
  6. Legal Aid commission legislation and published priority guidelines — purpose versus recorded outcome.
  7. Privacy and surveillance law — relevant to the covert-surveillance / interception allegations if a competent investigator ever tests them.

What this brief does not do

  • It does not treat git history or blockchain claims as proof of file bytes. See verification.html.
