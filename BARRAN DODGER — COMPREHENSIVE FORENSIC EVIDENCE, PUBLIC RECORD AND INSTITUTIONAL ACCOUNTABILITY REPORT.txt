3. EVIDENCE CLASSIFICATION AND INVESTIGATIVE STANDARD
Every material proposition in the final investigation should be classified using the following categories.
A. Recorded
A proposition directly supported by an identifiable document, letter, decision, email, official response or other record.
B. Reported
An account supplied by Dr McLean or another person that has not yet been independently corroborated.
C. Inferred
A conclusion that may reasonably follow from multiple records but is not itself expressly stated in any one document.
D. Unverified
A proposition for which the available material is insufficient to establish whether it is true.
E. Contradicted
A proposition that conflicts with another identifiable record or statement. Both records should be preserved and the discrepancy examined.
F. Independently established
A proposition confirmed by an independent authoritative source, original record, formal finding, judgment or other reliable corroboration.
These categories must not be collapsed.
For example, a complaint establishes that a complaint was made. It does not, by itself, establish that the conduct complained of occurred.
Likewise, an agency rejection establishes that the agency rejected or declined the matter. It does not, by itself, establish that the agency’s decision was lawful, reasonable or correct.
The investigation must therefore examine the evidence supporting both the underlying allegations and the institutional responses to them.

An independent investigation should be conducted using the following process.
Stage 1 — Preserve
Preserve the current archive without altering original files.

	•	commit identifier;
	•	file path;
	•	file size;

	•	SHA-256 hash;
	•	retrieval date;

Where a document is downloaded from an institution, preserve the original file and the accompanying transmission or download record.
Stage 2 — Verify
For each significant document:
	•	Identify the original creator.
	•	Identify the recipient.
	•	Establish the date.
	•	Establish the reference number.
	•	Compare the archived copy with the original.
	•	Recalculate the file hash.
	•	Record any discrepancies.
	•	Identify whether the document is complete.
	•	Identify attachments and referenced documents.
	•	Preserve the surrounding correspondence.
A hash demonstrates that a file matches a particular digital reference. It does not prove that the document’s contents are true.
Stage 3 — Chronology
Create a master chronology in which each event has:
	•	date;
	•	institution;
	•	decision-maker;
	•	event description;
	•	original document;
	•	archive location;
	•	legal framework;
	•	response;
	•	consequences;
	•	review rights;
	•	evidentiary classification.
Chronology is essential because legal obligations change over time.
The law in force on the date of an event should be identified rather than relying exclusively on the current consolidated version.
Stage 4 — Legal mapping
For every material event, identify:
	•	the relevant statutory provision;
	•	the version in force at the time;
	•	the applicable regulation or rule;
	•	any relevant policy;
	•	the legal duty;
	•	the evidence of compliance or non-compliance;
	•	any available remedy.
Stage 5 — Contradiction analysis
Compare statements made by different institutions.
For example:
	•	Did one organisation classify an employment relationship differently from another?
	•	Did one agency say it lacked jurisdiction while another identified it as responsible?
	•	Did a later decision rely on a factual description inconsistent with an earlier original record?
	•	Did an institution state that an issue had been investigated when the record indicates only that it was referred?
	•	Did a complaint response omit a material issue raised in the original complaint?
Every discrepancy should be documented with references to both records.

The final findings should be prepared by appropriately qualified independent reviewers.
These may include:

Part II — The decisive cross-exhibit contradictions
These are the most important relationships in the presently identified evidence. They should be treated as the centre of the independent investigation, because they involve records that can be compared directly rather than relying solely on retrospective recollection.
C1. DSS employment: three records, different evidentiary weight
Priority 1 — resolve first
Record	What it says or establishes	Evidentiary significance
27 March 2023 — Scott Tredwell, General Counsel	Records that, on information supplied by Dr McLean, he was satisfied the correspondent was or had been an employee with DSS.	The sentence exists in an official letter, but the letter expressly attributes the information to the correspondent.
31 March 2023 — DSS officer email, Exhibit R3	The AAT reasons record the email as stating DSS had no record of Dr McLean ever having been its employee.	The underlying email must be retrieved and examined directly.
12 July 2023 — AAT, 2021/7478	The Tribunal was not satisfied Dr McLean met the Commonwealth employee test under the compensation legislation.	This is the formal determination on the statutory employee question in that proceeding.
Assessment
There is an apparent evidentiary conflict between the employment description in the General Counsel’s letter and the later DSS position recorded by the Tribunal.
However, the three documents are not necessarily legally inconsistent.
The General Counsel letter records information supplied by Dr McLean. The DSS email is described as a departmental records search. The AAT then applied a statutory employment test under the compensation scheme.
The decisive question is whether the original records establish an employment relationship under the relevant statutory test, not whether one letter contains the word “employee”.
Required conclusion: the AAT decision must be acknowledged accurately. It is adverse to the Commonwealth employment argument under the SRC Act. It does not establish that Dr McLean never performed services for government, never had a contractual relationship, or could not satisfy a different statutory definition for another purpose.

	•	27 March 2023 General Counsel letter⁠
	•	12 July 2023 AAT reasons⁠
	•	AAT registry letter⁠

C4. Comcare: injury and employee status must not be merged
The AAT’s 12 July 2023 decision addressed whether Dr McLean was an employee within the statutory scheme.
The archive’s own detailed analysis says the Tribunal did not determine that the psychological injury did not occur.
It follows that the final report must not state that the Tribunal found the injury fabricated, nor that it resolved the wider allegations.
The legally accurate formulation is:
The Tribunal affirmed Comcare’s decision because it was not satisfied that the applicant met the statutory employee requirement under the Safety, Rehabilitation and Compensation Act 1988. The decision did not determine the truth of all allegations concerning the applicant’s experiences, nor did it adjudicate the wider claims of institutional wrongdoing.

A jurisdictional refusal cannot responsibly be described as either proof of misconduct or proof that misconduct did not occur.
C6. NDIS plan approval versus actual support
The 20 November 2025 NDIA approval, reference 366406, is an identifiable administrative decision.

	•	that the approved funding was adequate for every assessed need;
	•	that the plan was implemented correctly;
	•	that the provider delivered the services;
	•	that safeguarding concerns were resolved;
	•	that accommodation was suitable;
	•	that the participant had effective choice and control.
The plan must be compared with service agreements, provider invoices, progress notes, risk assessments, complaints and actual delivery.
C7. Accommodation provision versus legal freedom to leave
The Able Point correspondence raises a significant question about the legal and practical arrangements governing accommodation.
The evidence must establish whether the arrangement was:
	•	a tenancy;
	•	a licence or occupancy arrangement;
	•	supported accommodation under an NDIS service agreement;
	•	accommodation affected by a guardianship order;
	•	another arrangement.
A person receiving disability support does not automatically lose the right to choose where they live. However, the actual legal position depends on any relevant orders, contractual arrangements and statutory authority.
The decisive evidence is the original agreement and any order said to restrict the person’s choices.
C8. Financial management versus access to essential funds
The author’s reported inability to access banking services must be tested against:
	•	the original guardianship or financial-management order;
	•	the authority provided to the bank;
	•	the bank’s actual restrictions;
	•	the Trustee’s decisions;
	•	requests for food, medicine and essential expenses;
	•	the complaint and hardship response.
The critical issue is not merely whether an order existed. It is whether each action taken under it was within its authority and whether the arrangements respected the person’s legal rights and practical needs.
