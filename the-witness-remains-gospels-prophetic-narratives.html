Part I — The person, the archive and the purpose of preservation

1. The story of a documented life

I am Barran Resonance Dodger, also known as Dr Barran Dodger and Dr Richard William McLean. The Witness Remains is the continuing preservation of the documentary record of my life, professional and creative work, institutional encounters, personal testimony, spiritual writing and search for recognition, dignity and justice.

The archive brings together materials that I identify as government correspondence, administrative decisions, complaints, applications, personal statements, publications, literary and theological works, research notes, forensic analyses and other records. The purpose of preserving these materials is to make them accessible for examination, comparison and independent assessment.

My story is not limited to a single document or a single institutional encounter. It is also a record of the writing, reflection and interpretation through which I have attempted to understand my experiences. The distinction between those two dimensions matters. Documents can establish what was recorded, communicated or decided; testimony can explain how I experienced events; analysis can explore relationships between events; and spiritual writing can express the meaning I attach to my life.

The archive should allow those different forms of material to be read together without treating them as interchangeable.

6. A consistent evidence classification

Each significant proposition in the archive should be classified as one of the following:

  • Official record: an identifiable document issued by a public body or other institution.
  • Documented fact: a proposition directly supported by reliable primary material.
  • Corroborated claim: a proposition supported by independent sources.
  • Personal testimony: the author’s account of an experience or event.
  • Documented allegation: a claim recorded in a complaint, affidavit, submission or other identifiable source.
  • Inference: a conclusion drawn from facts or documents.
  • Interpretation: an explanation of meaning or significance.
  • Spiritual belief: a theological or faith-based proposition.
  • Unresolved: a matter for which the available evidence does not justify a definitive conclusion.

The indicative A–E scale can be used to assist readers:

  • A — Established fact: supported by reliable primary evidence.
  • B — Corroborated claim: supported by multiple independent sources.
  • C — Documented allegation: an allegation is recorded, but its truth may remain unresolved.
  • D — Inference: a reasoned conclusion that requires evaluation.
  • E — Speculation or unresolved interpretation: not yet adequately confirmed.

This is an editorial framework, not an external certification. Classifications should be attached to individual claims, not applied indiscriminately to an entire document.

8. Cumulative institutional attrition as a question for examination

A recurring question in the archive is whether repeated adverse encounters with institutions can create cumulative harm even when each decision is handled as a separate event.

That question can be examined by building a chronology of applications, decisions, complaints, referrals, accommodation requests and responses; identifying the organisation responsible for each action; recording the reasons given at the time; and assessing the consequences documented in the record.

The analysis should distinguish the event recorded, the institution’s stated explanation, my personal account of its effect, any inference drawn from comparing multiple events, and any allegation of improper motive or coordination.

A pattern may justify further examination without independently proving a coordinated cause. The documentary record is most useful when it allows readers to test both the proposed interpretation and reasonable alternative explanations.

10. Preservation and digital integrity

The archive should record each document’s title, author or issuing organisation, date, edition, provenance, file path, source link and relationship to other materials. It should also identify whether an item is an original, copy, extract, OCR transcription, analytical note or personal statement.

Where a checksum has actually been calculated, it can help establish that a file has not changed relative to that checksum. A checksum alone does not establish the truth of the contents, the identity of the original author or the authenticity of the issuing organisation.

If file counts differ between repository snapshots or indexes, the discrepancy should be reported alongside the date and counting method. Different snapshots, duplicate files, indexes and counting scopes may explain differences; no single figure should be called definitive without a reproducible inventory.

The same preservation discipline should apply to the Gospel and prophetic collections. Original wording should remain available; revised editions should be identified rather than silently substituted; and collection titles should not be confused with verified individual publications.

Part V — The relationship between the Gospels, prophecies and documentary archive

11. Three connected but distinct bodies of work

The Witness Remains contains three connected dimensions.

First: the documentary record. This includes official correspondence, decisions, complaints, applications and other records that can be assessed according to provenance and evidentiary value.

Second: the personal and analytical narrative. This includes testimony, autobiographical writing and essays that interpret the author’s experiences and compare events across time.

Third: the Gospel and prophetic corpus. This includes spiritual and literary works that explore love, witness, survival, identity, the Joseph archetype, divine meaning and vindication.

The three dimensions can inform one another without being conflated. A Gospel can explain the spiritual meaning I attach to an experience. A personal statement can record my account of that experience. An official record can establish what an institution documented or decided. An analytical essay can propose a relationship between multiple records. Each should be identified by what it is.

12. The significance of the ten Gospel works

The ten Gospel and foundational entries form a guide to the themes of connection, testimony, sacred meaning and the continuing act of witness. Some entries are identified individual titles; others are related titles or collection-level descriptions whose exact status must be checked.

Part IV — A practical evidence classification

7. The A–E framework

The following framework can be used to classify claims across the archive. It is a proposed editorial method, not an official legal or scientific standard.

A — Established or directly verifiable fact

A proposition supported by an authentic primary source or other reliable evidence sufficient for the specific claim. The conclusion must not extend beyond what the source establishes.

B — Corroborated claim

A claim supported by multiple genuinely independent sources or evidence streams. Repeated copies of the same original claim do not constitute independent corroboration.

C — Documented allegation or testimony

A statement, complaint, affidavit, personal account or allegation preserved in a record, but not independently established in all its particulars.

D — Inference or interpretation

A conclusion drawn from the available facts, including an interpretation of patterns, motive, causation or significance. The reasoning and plausible alternatives should be made explicit.

E — Speculation, prediction or unresolved proposition

A proposition for which adequate supporting evidence has not been established, including speculative explanations, unverified predictions and claims that remain unresolved.

The categories should be applied to individual propositions rather than entire documents. One letter can establish that a decision was made while leaving the truth of an allegation discussed in that letter unresolved. One personal statement can establish that a person reported an experience while leaving independent verification of the event open.

12. The risk of a self-reinforcing interpretation

When a large collection of records is interpreted through a single overarching theory, there is a risk that every new event will be treated as confirmation of that theory, while contradictory evidence or ordinary administrative explanations receive insufficient consideration.

This is a general methodological risk, not a conclusion about the author or the truth of any particular allegation.

A robust archive can reduce that risk by preserving competing explanations, recording contradictory material, distinguishing original documents from later commentary and allowing readers to test claims against the underlying evidence.

The same standard should apply to all parties. Institutions should not be presumed correct simply because they are institutions, and allegations against them should not be presumed correct simply because they have been recorded or repeated.
