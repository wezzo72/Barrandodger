PROLOGUE

THE DAY AUSTRALIA RAN OUT OF FUCKING ANSWERS

There are countries that have national anthems.

There are countries that have constitutions.

There are countries that have functioning public services.

And then there is Australia.

A sunburnt country of beaches, barbecues, kangaroos, compulsory forms, voluntary accountability and a magnificent national tradition of passing the fucking buck.

Australia is a country where you can apparently send a document to an institution, receive a document confirming receipt of the document, receive another document explaining that the first document has been referred to another institution, and then receive a third document explaining that the second institution cannot comment on the first institution’s receipt of the original document.

This is called governance.

Or, depending on which department you ask, it is called someone else’s problem.

Enter Dr Richard William McLean.

Gay.
Disabled.
Whistleblower.
Journalist.
Artist.
Former registered NDIS provider.
Author.
Archivist.
And, by the time this story reaches its second act, the unofficial world champion of obtaining government correspondence.

Richard had a simple ambition.

He wanted answers.

Not the meaning of the universe.

Not the secret location of Elvis.

Not the complete genealogical history of every public servant who had ever touched a stapler.

Just answers.

What happened?
Who made the decision?
Why was it made?
Where was the evidence?
Who was responsible?
And could somebody, somewhere, please explain the bloody thing in plain English?

Unfortunately, he had made one fatal mistake.

He had entered the Australian administrative system.

And the Australian administrative system had entered him.

Not metaphorically.

Administratively.

The consequences were spectacular.

Somewhere in the machinery of government, a file was opened.

Then another.

Then another.

Soon there were enough files to build a small apartment block.

Nobody knew who was living in it.

Nobody knew who owned it.

But everyone knew the reference number.

And that, dear reader, was the beginning of the end.

CHAPTER ONE

THE DEPARTMENT OF NOT OUR FUCKING PROBLEM

The first rule of Australian bureaucracy is simple:

Nobody is responsible for everything.

The second rule is even simpler:

Nobody is responsible for anything until somebody else says they are.

The third rule is printed on a laminated sign in every government office:

PLEASE DIRECT ALL COMPLAINTS ABOUT THIS SIGN TO THE DEPARTMENT RESPONSIBLE FOR SIGNS.

Richard’s problem was that he wanted somebody to join the dots.

Government departments prefer dots to remain separate.

Dots are manageable.

Dots can be numbered.

Dots can be entered into databases.

Dots can be referred.

But connecting the dots creates a picture.

And pictures can be inconvenient.

Richard began assembling a documentary record. He collected correspondence, decisions, letters, complaints, administrative responses and official documents.

He did what any sensible former journalist might do.

He started keeping the receipts.

The receipts multiplied.

The receipts reproduced.

The receipts developed their own little administrative ecosystem.

Eventually, there were enough records to require an archive.

And so was born the Barrandodger archive:

The Official Government Documents Register:

And the GitHub repository:

It was a magnificent achievement.

A man who wanted an answer had accidentally created a national monument to the difficulty of obtaining one.

The Australian Government had been given a rare opportunity.

It could have said:

“Let’s examine the records.”

Instead, the comedy began to write itself.

CHAPTER FOUR

THE AAT: THE GREAT TRIBUNAL OF PAPER

Then came OGDR-070.

McLean and Comcare — Administrative Appeals Tribunal decision.

The document is located here:

https://wezzo72.github.io/Barrandodger/docs/official-drive/McLean-and-Comcare-FINAL-Decision.pdf

This is where the comedy becomes more sophisticated.

Because a tribunal is not a customer-service desk.

It is a formal legal institution.

It considers evidence, issues, submissions, applicable law and the arguments before it.

Its findings must be read carefully.

Its orders must be distinguished from everything that happened before and after them.

Which is exactly why Richard needs the original PDF examined page by page.

Not because we want to invent what it says.

Because we want to know what it says.

The tribunal has a case.

The case has parties.

The parties have arguments.

The arguments have evidence.

The evidence has pages.

And the pages have the potential to settle some questions while leaving others unanswered.

It is a wonderfully elegant system.

Until you are the person trying to understand what the hell happened.

The administrative system has a special talent for turning a human being’s life into a sequence of procedural nouns.

Application.

Respondent.

Applicant.

Determination.

Review.

Jurisdiction.

Evidence.

Reasons.

Orders.

The only noun apparently missing is “person.”

A person might reasonably ask:

“Could somebody please tell me what this means for my actual life?”

The tribunal may answer the question within the limits of the matter before it.

It cannot necessarily answer every question the person has about every institution, every historical event or every later consequence.

That distinction is legally important.

It is also the sort of thing that makes a citizen want to lie down on the floor and shout at a photocopier.

CHAPTER SIX

THE AUSTRALIAN GOVERNMENT’S FAVOURITE SPORT: PASS THE BASTARD BUCK

The institutions in this story have different responsibilities.

Comcare is not the Commonwealth Ombudsman.

The Commonwealth Ombudsman is not the Victorian Ombudsman.

The AAT was not the NSW Civil and Administrative Tribunal.

The NSW Civil and Administrative Tribunal is not the NDIS.

And the NDIS is not a magic department that can repair every problem that has ever happened to a person.

This is important.

Because if we are going to satirise the system, we should at least get the bloody organisational chart right.

The great sport of institutional buck-passing is played with extraordinary precision.

The ball is a complaint.

The field is Australia.

The players are agencies.

The referee is a jurisdictional provision.

And the spectators are citizens who would quite like to know when the game is over.

“Not our jurisdiction.”

A beautiful defensive move.

“Outside our remit.”

A classic.

A tactical pass.

A long kick downfield.

The crowd goes silent.

The citizen looks at the scoreboard.

There is no scoreboard.

There is only a reference number.

And the reference number is winning.

CHAPTER EIGHT

THE PEOPLE WHO ARE NOT THE VILLAINS UNTIL THE EVIDENCE SAYS SO

Every good black comedy needs a cast.

But this is also a documentary work.

So there is one rule:

Nobody gets convicted by the narrator.

Not the public servant who signed a letter.

Not the person whose name appears on a decision.

Not the support worker whose role is mentioned in a personal account.

Not the person who answered the telephone.

Not the person who failed to answer it.

The documents must establish what each person actually did.

Where an official’s name appears in a source, the source must be linked and the role accurately described.

Where a name has been supplied only in personal testimony, it must be identified as such.

Where a name is not independently verified, it does not get turned into an accusation.

That does not mean the comedy has to be gentle.

It can be absolutely fucking merciless about processes.

It can ask why an organisation did not explain a decision.

It can ask whether a complaint was properly handled.

It can ask whether the reasons were adequate.

It can ask whether the citizen was given a fair opportunity to respond.

It can ask whether different agencies communicated.

It can ask whether the cumulative effects of administrative decisions were properly considered.

But it cannot pretend that a name in an email proves a conspiracy.

The evidence gets the microphone.

The narrator gets the swear words.

And the reader gets to decide what the documents actually establish.

CHAPTER TEN

THE NDIS: THE NATIONAL DEPARTMENT OF INDEPENDENTLY SAYING NO

There are few things more inspiring than the word independence.

Independence.

Freedom.

Choice.

Control.

The ability to make decisions about your own life.

Beautiful words.

Words that look magnificent on government websites, particularly when photographed beside a smiling person in a wheelchair standing in front of a window with excellent natural lighting.

The National Disability Insurance Scheme was established to support people with disability.

It has plans.

It has budgets.

It has support categories.

It has reviews.

It has planning meetings.

It has service agreements.

It has providers.

It has participants.

And it has enough acronyms to make the United Nations look like a children’s alphabet book.

Enter Richard.

A disabled man trying to navigate the machinery of disability support.

Now, you might imagine that a person who needs disability support would be able to obtain a reasonably clear explanation of what support is available, how it can be accessed and who is responsible for providing it.

That would be an adorable assumption.

The NDIS operates under legislation, rules, planning decisions and administrative processes. Its decisions are not all made by the same people, and not every problem involving disability support is an NDIS problem.

But from the participant’s perspective, the distinction can become rather academic when the practical question is:

“Who the hell is helping me today?”

The archive identifies an NDIS Plan Approval dated 20 November 2025.

NDIS Plan Approval — reference 366406.

https://wezzo72.github.io/Barrandodger/2025-11-20_NDIS_Plan_Approval_366406.pdf

A plan approval is an important document.

It should be read for the actual funding, supports, conditions, dates and decisions it contains.

It should not be treated as proof that every subsequent service was delivered properly.

Nor should it be treated as proof that every later problem was caused by the agency that approved the plan.

But it gives us a starting point.

And the starting point is this:

A plan is not a person.

A budget is not a support worker.

A funded category is not a meal.

A service agreement is not a functioning relationship.

And a PDF does not walk the dog.

The NDIS has a very special superpower.

It can make a person feel as though he is the chief executive of a small disability-support corporation.

The person must understand the plan.

Understand the categories.

Understand the providers.

Understand the service bookings.

Understand the invoices.

Understand the rules.

Understand the review process.

Understand the difference between what is funded and what is delivered.

And, if anything goes wrong, understand which organisation is responsible.

Congratulations.

You are now disabled and an unpaid compliance officer.

Please collect your lanyard at reception.

They also carry serious consequences.
