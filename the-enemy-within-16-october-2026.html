PART ONE — THE FAMILY, THE COMMUNITY, AND THE ORIGINS OF EXILE

1. The Story That Must Be Told in Full

My story cannot be understood by examining individual incidents in isolation. To understand what happened to me, readers need to understand the family into which I was born, the people who held social and financial influence, the expectations of loyalty and masculinity that surrounded me, and the difference between the public reputations of my relatives and the private experiences I remember.

I have spent years trying to understand how I came to experience such profound isolation from my family and community, particularly during periods when I was vulnerable, financially insecure, homeless, or trying to obtain assistance after injury.

I have questioned why the people who appeared to possess resources, influence, professional relationships, and community standing did not provide the kind of practical support I believed was available to them when I needed it.

I have questioned why my isolation was sometimes treated as a personal failing rather than a circumstance deserving compassion and investigation.

I have questioned why I felt that my sexuality was a source of shame, why experiences from my youth continued to affect my understanding of myself, and why I struggled to obtain a hearing when I attempted to explain what I had experienced.

I have also questioned the relationship between family loyalty and silence. I was taught that family mattered, that relatives were meant to stand together, and that loyalty was an important virtue. For a long time, I held on to those beliefs and continued to hope that my parents and extended family would eventually understand my circumstances.

Over time, I began to question whether my understanding of loyalty had left me vulnerable to accepting treatment that I should have challenged much earlier.

This document records that process of reflection.

It is not written on the assumption that every relative shared the same motives, that every disagreement was coordinated, or that every suspicion I have formed is necessarily correct. It is written because my recollections deserve to be recorded in their full context, because the relevant documentary evidence should be open to examination, and because the distinction between a person’s public standing and another person’s private experience can be significant.

My purpose is to preserve the history as I remember it, identify the questions that remain unresolved, and invite examination of the evidence rather than allowing my account to be reduced to a convenient characterisation of me.

I want readers to understand the circumstances in which my sense of belonging deteriorated, the relationships I came to distrust, and the reasons I believe family, community reputation, financial power, and institutional responses must be examined together.

2. Snowy McLean: A Popular Grandfather and the Family Culture I Came to Question

My grandfather, Snowy McLean, occupies an important place in my understanding of the

family culture in which I grew up.

As I remember him, Grandpa was a popular, clever, cheeky, and mischievous man. He had personality. He was socially recognised, and he knew how to occupy a place within the world around him. He could be entertaining and memorable, and his reputation was part of the family history that surrounded me.

I also understand that he was a member of the Freemasons and held a senior Masonic title that I remember as “Grand Puja.” I want that title to be checked against reliable historical or organisational records before it is represented as the precise formal title he held.

At the time, I did not understand the significance that his membership, social relationships, and standing might have had within the wider culture of our family and community. I was a younger person, and I did not have the knowledge or perspective to examine those things critically.

I have since become concerned about what I perceive as the capacity of closed social networks to reinforce loyalty, protect reputations, discourage dissent, and make it difficult for people who do not conform to prevailing expectations to be accepted.

I have also come to associate aspects of the culture I experienced with rigid expectations of masculinity, hostility towards homosexuality, and an emphasis on loyalty that can become harmful when it is used to discourage a person from speaking about mistreatment.

My personal spiritual interpretation of these experiences has become strong. I have sometimes described what I perceive as the darker or corrupt aspects of that culture in terms of spiritual corruption, including language about satanic influence. That is my religious and moral interpretation of the experiences I have described; I do not have evidence establishing that Freemasonry as a whole is satanic, or that every member shares the attitudes I experienced.

My concern is not to substitute a label for evidence. It is to explain why I have come to question the values, conduct, and social expectations that shaped my experience of family life.

One memory, in particular, has stayed with me.

Grandpa used to call me a “poofter.”

That word was not neutral in the environment in which I grew up. It was a derogatory term directed at perceived homosexuality or a failure to conform to conventional masculine expectations. At a time when I was not confident about my sexuality, being called that by a grandfather whose approval and acceptance mattered to me was painful.

Nana was upset when he called me that. She would ask him not to do it.

Her reaction is important to my account. It demonstrates that the language was not necessarily accepted by everyone in the family and that someone close to me recognised its hurtful character. Nana’s objections stand out in my memory because she was trying to stop a form of treatment that caused me distress.

I remember that distinction.

Grandpa could use the word, but Nana recognised that it was wrong to continue using it against me.

I cannot establish precisely what Grandpa intended each time he said it. He may have understood it as teasing, or he may have held attitudes about homosexuality that I can no longer independently determine. What I can describe is how I remember the words and the effect they had on me.

I was growing up at a time when I was still trying to understand my sexuality and find confidence in myself. Instead of feeling entirely free to explore who I was, I became conscious that other people might interpret my behaviour through a derogatory label.

I began to wonder whether being different meant that I would be ridiculed, judged, or treated as someone whose dignity mattered less.

Looking back, I believe those experiences may have contributed to the atmosphere in which my sexuality was understood within the family. I also wonder whether the attitudes expressed by Grandpa influenced my father’s perspective. I cannot prove the extent of that influence, and I do not claim to know every private conversation that occurred between them. But the possibility is important to my understanding of the family dynamics that followed.

A child’s understanding of acceptance is shaped by repeated experiences. When a person hears a derogatory term used against them by a family member, and when that term relates to a part of themselves they are still struggling to understand, the experience can become more than a passing insult.

It can shape their expectations of other people’s reactions.

It can make them reluctant to speak openly.

It can cause them to anticipate humiliation before it occurs.

And it can leave them uncertain whether the people who claim to love them will defend their dignity when it matters.

I believe this history deserves to be considered when examining my later experiences of sexuality-based shame, isolation, and conflict with people in positions of authority.

That detail matters because it demonstrates that the treatment I remember was not something I have retrospectively invented simply because I later became unhappy with my family circumstances. It is a specific recollection of a repeated form of language and of another family member’s objection to it.

Nana’s reaction also reminds me that people within the same family can understand a situation differently. One person may dismiss hurtful language as humour, while another recognises the humiliation it can cause.

I do not know whether Nana fully understood the broader effect those words had on my developing confidence and sense of belonging. But I remember that she did not want Grandpa to continue calling me that.

Her objection gave me an indication that the language was not inevitable, necessary, or beyond criticism.

Someone could challenge it.

Someone could say that it should stop.

That is significant because much of my later experience involved trying to understand why conduct I found hurtful seemed to remain unacknowledged or unchallenged by

people whose support I wanted.

In my understanding, a healthy family should be able to recognise when teasing becomes humiliation, when loyalty becomes silence, and when a person’s vulnerability calls for protection rather than ridicule.

I wanted that kind of protection. I wanted to be accepted without having to prove that I was sufficiently masculine, sufficiently successful, or sufficiently convenient to other people’s reputations.

I wanted to be able to grow into adulthood without feeling that my sexuality made me a target.

Nana’s objection remains one of the memories through which I understand that a different response was possible.

4. My Father: Fifty Years of Baseball, Community Recognition, and Social Standing

My father held an important place in the local community through baseball.

As I remember it, he was a popular and proud member of the local baseball club. He devoted approximately fifty years to baseball and to the club, and I understand that the club was named after him while he was still alive.

That was a substantial public legacy.

Dad was not simply someone who occasionally participated in a recreational activity. Baseball was a major part of his life and public identity. His long involvement meant that he was known by many people, and he had relationships extending beyond the immediate circle of players.

In a community connected through sport, employment, business, and local institutions, a long-serving club member can become familiar to people across many parts of society.

I understood Dad to be someone who knew just about everyone around him.

His circle may have included lawyers, police officers, firefighters, tradespeople, business owners, other sporting figures, and people who were socially connected through their work and community involvement. I cannot independently establish the precise extent of every relationship or what any particular person knew about my

private life. But my understanding was that Dad had a substantial network of acquaintances and longstanding community relationships.

His public standing matters to my story because it forms part of the contrast I experienced between his place in the community and my own.

Dad was a recognised person. His contribution to baseball was known and celebrated. He had a community identity built over decades.

I did not feel that I had an equivalent place.

I remember spending substantial periods of my youth driving around in the car, often alone. Rather than feeling securely connected to family and community, I experienced significant isolation. I was still trying to understand myself and work out where I belonged.

I remember Dad sometimes saying words to the effect of:

“My mate saw the Volkswagen down South Road. What were you doing down there? Off on your own again?”

I remember the effect of those comments. I felt that I was being questioned or berated for being alone, as though my movements were something to criticise rather than a sign that I was isolated.

The question that has remained with me is why my being alone seemed to attract scrutiny without, in my experience, leading to a meaningful examination of why I was spending so much time alone in the first place.

I felt that I was being held responsible for my isolation.

But I did not experience that isolation as something I had simply chosen without cause. I felt excluded. I felt uncertain about my place in the family. I felt that I was trying to navigate a world in which my sexuality and identity could become subjects of judgement.

The contradiction was painful: my father could be celebrated for his contribution to the local community, while I felt that I was spending my youth outside the security of that same community.

I do not claim that Dad’s popularity proves he used his influence against me. Nor do I

claim that being a respected member of a sporting club makes a person responsible for every action taken by other club members or community figures.

The significance is different.

His standing meant that he was socially embedded in a network from which I often felt disconnected. It made me conscious of the difference between being known and respected and feeling that one’s own account might not receive the same recognition.

I wondered whether people who knew Dad would see events through the lens of his reputation. I wondered whether gossip about me would have a different meaning because of his position. I wondered whether the embarrassment I felt was connected to the prospect that private events might become known to people who had relationships with my family.

These questions became especially important in relation to the police incident I remember.

5. The Volkswagen, South Road, and the Contradiction of Being Questioned About Isolation

The memory of Dad referring to his mate seeing my Volkswagen on South Road has remained significant because of the way I experienced the exchange.

I remember being questioned about where I had been and why I was driving around alone. The comments conveyed to me that my movements were visible to people who knew my father and that those observations could be reported back to him.

The experience made me conscious that I was not moving through the community as an entirely anonymous young person. I felt that I could be recognised, discussed, and questioned through the social relationships surrounding my family.

That feeling was particularly uncomfortable because I was already struggling with isolation and uncertainty about my identity.

When Dad asked what I was doing down there or why I was “off on my own again,” I felt that the focus was on my behaviour rather than my circumstances.

I wanted someone to ask why I was alone so often.

I wanted someone to consider whether I was feeling excluded or whether there were

reasons I did not feel comfortable within the family and community.

I wanted to be offered understanding rather than made to feel that my solitude was evidence of some personal defect.

I experienced the questioning as a form of contradiction. I felt that I was isolated by the circumstances of my family life, yet I was also criticised for being isolated.

I cannot establish that Dad intended to create this contradiction. He may have believed that he was asking ordinary parental questions or expressing concern. I am recording how I remember those conversations and why they became important in the way I later interpreted my relationship with him.

The distinction between intention and impact is essential.

I cannot read another person’s mind. I can, however, describe the impact of a repeated interaction on my own understanding of myself.

The impact was that I felt watched but not understood.

I felt questioned but not necessarily supported.

I felt that people might know where I had been without knowing why I was there.

And I felt that the community in which my father was respected was not necessarily a community in which I could comfortably explain my own experience.

That is the context in which I remember the later police incident.

At the time, I was not confident about my sexuality. I was still trying to understand myself and determine how I could live without being humiliated or judged for aspects of my identity that I did not yet fully understand.

The incident became significant in my memory because it was connected to the possibility of exposure, community gossip, and sexuality-based shame.

I remember the encounter as consensual. I believe it is important to preserve that distinction rather than allow the fact that police became involved to determine how the event is automatically interpreted.

The exact circumstances, including what occurred, what the police observed, what was said, and how the matter was handled, should be assessed against any available contemporaneous records or independent evidence. What I remember most clearly about the broader experience is the sense of vulnerability.

I was young and uncertain about my sexuality. I was conscious that other people might interpret my behaviour through a derogatory label. I already had memories of Grandpa calling me a “poofter” and Nana objecting. I cannot independently establish what every person involved believed. I cannot say, without evidence, that every officer shared the same attitude or that the entire Springvale Police Force acted according to a single intention.

But I can describe the question that has remained with me: was I being treated as a young person whose circumstances required fair and respectful handling, or was my sexuality becoming a source of humiliation?

That question matters because a person’s experience of an encounter with authority can be shaped by more than the immediate event. It can also be shaped by fear, stigma, power imbalance, and the possibility that information will spread through a close-knit community.

I was particularly conscious of my father’s social standing. He had spent decades involved in baseball and was widely known through the club. That made the possibility of gossip especially confronting.

I feared that I would be shamed not because the encounter had been non-consensual, but because people might attach a negative meaning to my sexuality, my masculinity, or my perceived failure to conform to expectations.

My father had an established public identity. He was known through decades of participation in baseball. I did not have the same standing, confidence, or ability to influence how other people might interpret events involving me.

I felt that I was vulnerable to being discussed while lacking the social position to answer that discussion on equal terms.

The contrast was not simply between a popular father and an isolated son. It was between two different levels of social security.

Dad had a recognised place in the community.

I was still trying to find a place where I could be accepted.

Dad’s history in baseball was publicly associated with commitment and community service.

My private life, as I feared it might be interpreted, could become a source of embarrassment or ridicule.

I cannot prove that other people deliberately used this imbalance against me. But the imbalance forms an important part of how I remember the circumstances and why I believe they deserve examination.

I came to see him as a patriarch within the family: someone whose resources and approval mattered, whose actions could affect the opportunities available to relatives, and whose position carried social weight.

I do not suggest that being wealthy or influential is inherently wrong. Nor do I suggest that providing employment or financial assistance necessarily creates improper control. My concern is how these relationships operated in the context of the circumstances I experienced.
